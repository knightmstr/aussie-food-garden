# Sources, limits and updates

## How this guide was made

The research was done in November 2026 using web searches and the pages those searches returned. The findings were then written up as these pages, with AI assistance (Claude), and every page was built from saved research notes rather than from memory. It has not been field-tested, and it is not a substitute for advice from your state agriculture department, a local gardening group or a qualified adviser.

## What the labels mean

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Linked source | The claim comes from the linked page |
| `[inference]` | Reasoning from sources, not stated by a source |
| `[general knowledge]` | Background knowledge not checked against a source. Never used for months, rates, product names or registration status |
| Strong / moderate / weak | The strength of the evidence behind a method (see below) |
| Proxy | A calendar or fact from a different place or climate type, shown because nothing local could be read |
| Folklore | A popular claim with no supporting evidence found |

## How evidence is graded

- **Strong:** government or university trials, product labels, or several agreeing government sources.
- **Moderate:** a single trial, or a government source with a clear limit (for example a broadacre result or a glasshouse trial).
- **Weak or anecdotal:** practitioner guides, blogs, newspaper columns, retailer pages and home remedies. These are still shown where useful, and labelled.

## Source types you will see

Pages name the type of each source: government, council, research body, industry body, community group, commercial (nurseries, seed sellers, product makers) or secondary (such as Wikipedia). Commercial and secondary sources are used only where nothing better could be read, and are labelled.

## Known limits

- **Planting calendars are the weakest part.** Most official calendars are images or PDF grids whose month columns did not extract as text. Where that happened, the page says so and shows only sourced months, or labelled proxies. The official Darwin/Katherine grid (NT Information Sheet IS4) is the clearest example. If you can read it, please send the months as a contribution.
- **Fruit and nut trees:** only trees with at least one sourced fact have a row, so many common trees are listed under *Gaps*.
- **Native foods:** only part of the requested species list was verified. The rest are listed as gaps, not guessed.
- **Pest and disease products:** registration for home use was not checked in the APVMA database. Product names appear only where a source printed them. Always follow the label or permit.
- **Rules change.** Fruit fly zones, movement restrictions and declared weed lists change often. Treat every rule here as a pointer to the agency to ask.
- **Regional coverage is uneven.** For example, there is almost nothing for the Wheatbelt, western Queensland, the Tasmanian north, or inland NSW.

## Updating this guide

The pages live in `docs/` as markdown in the GitHub repository. To correct a fact or add a better source, edit the page and open a pull request (see `CONTRIBUTING.md`). The build checks links and menu entries automatically. Useful next sources, which turned up in searches but could not be read, include the NT Information Sheet IS4 and IS5 calendar grids, Business Queensland and council calendars, BoM climate statistics for frost dates, the Hort Innovation cover-crop report VG16068, and the NSW and Victorian declared-weed lists.
