# Rotation, soil and companions

> **How reliable is this page?** Rotation by plant family rests on practitioner consensus (moderate evidence), not trials. The nitrogen numbers for legumes are real but come mostly from broadacre farms, sugar cane fallows and vineyards, so garden results will differ. Several results are one trial (the Katherine sunn hemp trial, one Gatton biofumigation trial) or glasshouse only (nematode screening). Most companion-planting evidence is from overseas; the Australian trials are for brassicas. Where sources disagree, both figures are shown. Items tagged [inference] are reasoning from the sources, and [general knowledge] is not source-checked.

## The short version

- Rotate by plant family: no same-family crops back to back, no heavy feeder after a heavy feeder, and a green manure at least once every four seasons ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)).
- A legume's nitrogen benefit is real but brief, about one crop. Put a hungry crop straight after it ([GRDC 2009](https://grdc.com.au/Research-and-Development/GRDC-Update-Papers/2009/09/NITROGEN-FIXATION-BENEFITS-OF-PULSE-CROPS)).
- Choose soil-builders by climate: bulk grasses in the Top End, winter legumes in the southern and dry zones. See [Which soil-builder for which climate](#which-soil-builder-for-which-climate).
- Cut or dig in covers at the start of flowering, before seed set.
- Clubroot spores last up to 20 years, so a home rotation cannot clear them. Keep brassicas and brassica covers out of infected beds ([Hort Innovation VG15010](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-clubroot-management-in-brassica-vegetables-fact-sheet.pdf)).
- Companion planting: flowering insectary strips and trap crops have the best support. Basil improving tomato flavour is folklore. See [Companion planting](#companion-planting-evidence-versus-folklore).
- Test your soil before liming or adding gypsum. Acidity and sodicity are different problems.

## Rotation by plant family

Australian practitioner guidance groups crops by family and moves each group to a new bed each cycle. Evidence: moderate (consensus, not trials). Sustainable Gardening Australia (SGA) lists these families ([SGA Crop rotation](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)).

| Family | Examples |
|---|---|
| Amaranthaceae | beetroot, quinoa, spinach, chard |
| Cucurbitaceae | cucumber, zucchini, melon, pumpkin, squash |
| Solanaceae | eggplant, capsicum/chilli, potato, tomato |
| Asteraceae | lettuce, chicory/endive, Jerusalem artichoke, salsify |
| Apiaceae | carrot, celeriac, celery, fennel, parsley, parsnip, dill |
| Brassicaceae | broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, kale, kohlrabi, mustard, Asian brassicas, radish, swede, turnip |
| Fabaceae | beans, peas, alfalfa, clover, fenugreek, lupin |
| Alliaceae | garlic, leek, onion, shallot |
| Poaceae | sweet corn, maize |

- **SGA's rules:** no same-family crops back to back; no heavy feeder after a heavy feeder; a green manure at least once every four seasons. Many pests and diseases are host-specific, so moving the host away stops them building up ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)).
- **Heavy feeders** (SGA): potato, tomato, cauliflower, broccoli, cabbage, sweet corn, lettuce, cucumber, zucchini, spinach, Asian greens. **Light feeders:** onion, leek, garlic, beetroot, carrot, parsnip, silverbeet (said to do poorly in rich soil, so follow heavy feeders). **Soil builders:** legumes (peas, snow peas, broad, runner and snake beans) plus green manures ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)).
- **Caveat:** the SGA page contradicts itself in places (lettuce is listed as both a daisy-family crop and a heavy feeder; silverbeet is a light feeder though it is in the same family as spinach). It gives no 3-year rotation and no climate detail ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)).
- **How long a gap:** a seed retailer says 3-4 years before a family returns is a guideline that is often impractical in small gardens. It notes that tomato and potato share early and late blight, that root-knot nematodes (RKN) can build under tomato, potato and cucurbits, and that rotation does not control wind-, water- or insect-borne disease ([Happy Valley Seeds](https://www.happyvalleyseeds.com.au/blogs/crop-rotation/crop-rotation-home-garden); commercial source).
- **Clubroot is the exception.** Spores persist up to 20 years. The industry fact sheet recommends more than 7 years between brassica crops, and a minimum of 2 years only inside an integrated strategy. Lime to pH 7.0-7.5 reduces the impact (apply at least a week before transplanting); pH below 6 raises the risk. Weed hosts include shepherd's purse and wild radish. The sheet lists brassica cover crops (it names Caliente mustard and Nemat rocket) as raising risk ([Hort Innovation VG15010](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-clubroot-management-in-brassica-vegetables-fact-sheet.pdf)). Evidence: strong (levy-funded industry research). A home rotation cannot solve clubroot; lime plus keeping brassicas, radish and mustard covers out of infected beds is the practical response [inference].
- **Nematodes:** root-knot nematodes (RKN) can build under tomato, potato and cucurbits ([Happy Valley Seeds](https://www.happyvalleyseeds.com.au/blogs/crop-rotation/crop-rotation-home-garden)). In Alice Springs, tomato, okra, cucumber and zucchini are the most affected, and brassicas, garlic and onion are more tolerant. Bare fallow over six months can cut some populations if kept weed-free, but "may not reduce numbers below damage thresholds"; cool-season planting and added organic matter are the other non-chemical options, each with limited evidence ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/?p=85173); [Alice Springs guide](https://assets.nationbuilder.com/aridlands/pages/4419/attachments/original/1726025047/Vegie_companion_small2.pdf)).

## A simple 4-bed rotation

SGA's 4-bed, 4-season cycle is **Legume, then Heavy feeder, then Light feeder, then Green manure**, with the beds staggered so each season has one of each ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)). Laid out as a grid [inference from SGA's cycle]:

| Season | Bed 1 | Bed 2 | Bed 3 | Bed 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Legume | Heavy feeder | Light feeder | Green manure |
| 2 | Heavy feeder | Light feeder | Green manure | Legume |
| 3 | Light feeder | Green manure | Legume | Heavy feeder |
| 4 | Green manure | Legume | Heavy feeder | Light feeder |

- **Example crops:** legume bed, peas or broad, runner or snake beans. Heavy bed, tomato, brassicas, sweet corn, cucurbits. Light bed, onion, garlic, carrot, beetroot. Green manure bed, a thickly sown annual grass and/or legume tilled in before flowering or seeding ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)).
- **Brassicas:** they are heavy feeders, but because of clubroot they need their own much longer cycle (see above), not a slot in the 4-bed grid [inference].
- **A 3-bed version** [inference from SGA's rules, not a sourced plan]: heavy feeders, then light feeders and roots, then a legume or green manure, repeated across three beds.
- **Why a hungry crop goes right after the legume:** the nitrogen benefit lasts about one season ([GRDC 2009](https://grdc.com.au/Research-and-Development/GRDC-Update-Papers/2009/09/NITROGEN-FIXATION-BENEFITS-OF-PULSE-CROPS)).
- **Top End version:** the NT vegetable manual's sequence is a leafy crop, then beans, then cucurbits, with a green manure in the rotation every wet season. It says legume green manures are susceptible to root-knot nematodes and should not precede susceptible vegetables ([NT vegetable growing manual](https://industry.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/228007/ntg-vegetable-growing-manual.pdf)). A working scaffold built from that manual and Agnote 636: wet-season grass green manure, then dry-season vegetables, then back to green manure; legume covers only where nematodes are not an issue [inference]. See [Northern Territory](states/nt.md).
- **A general suggestion** (from one source) to run the cycle by season in the wet-dry tropics, with a wet-season legume such as cowpea or sunn hemp, conflicts with the NT advice above [inference in the research notes]. For the Top End, follow the NT advice.

## What to plant after each crop

Every crop takes nutrients from the bed. Very little comes back by itself. What gives the soil something back is a legume (it fixes nitrogen), a green manure (a cover crop cut and dug in or left as mulch), or compost. So the question is what to grow next so the bed recovers instead of running down. This table uses [SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/), a [Salisbury Council sheet](https://www.salisbury.sa.gov.au/assets/files/assets/public/general_documents/live/healthy_living/living_well_in_the_living_city/pack_d5_crop_rotation.pdf), the [NT manual](https://industry.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/228007/ntg-vegetable-growing-manual.pdf), [Agnote 636](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0003/232968/636.pdf) and the green manure and nitrogen figures in the section below.

**The sourced rules of thumb**
- Do not follow a heavy feeder with another heavy feeder. [SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/crop-rotation/)'s order is legume, then heavy feeder, then light feeder, then green manure. Salisbury's is heavy, then moderate, then legume or root crop. Both put a recovery step (a light feeder, then a legume or green manure) after the hungry crop.
- A legume's nitrogen helps about one following crop, so put a hungry crop right after it ([GRDC 2009](https://grdc.com.au/Research-and-Development/GRDC-Update-Papers/2009/09/NITROGEN-FIXATION-BENEFITS-OF-PULSE-CROPS)).
- A bean or pea crop you pick returns less nitrogen than a green manure, because 30 to 40 percent of the crop's nitrogen sits in the seed and leaves with the harvest ([SA Soil Quality](https://www.soilquality.org.au/factsheets/legumes-and-nitrogen-fixation-south-australia)).
- Cut or dig in a cover at the start of flowering, then wait 4 to 6 weeks before sowing, or leave it as mulch and plant seedlings ([SGA green manure](https://sgaonline.org.au/green-manure)).
- In the Top End, use a bulk grass such as sorghum or pearl millet as the wet-season green manure, and leave the residue in place. Legume covers host root-knot nematodes ([NT manual](https://industry.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/228007/ntg-vegetable-growing-manual.pdf); [Agnote 636](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0003/232968/636.pdf)).
- In temperate areas like Sydney, winter-growing legumes suit best (faba beans, field peas, vetch and oats are listed). Summer covers often disappoint ([SGA green manure](https://sgaonline.org.au/green-manure); [Soil CRC](https://soilcrc.com.au/?p=6583)). Avoid mustard covers where clubroot is a risk.

## Legumes and green manures: what the numbers say

All figures below are from broadacre farms, cane fallows or vineyards unless stated. No garden-scale conversion is in the sources.

- **Fixation scales with shoot biomass.** Roughly 20 kg N per tonne of shoot dry matter (DM) for pulses, about 35 for annual clovers and about 37 for lucerne. These are derived from table values that the source's page treats inconsistently, so call them approximate. At 4 t/ha shoot DM: chickpea 87, field pea and faba 80, lupin 80, annual clovers 140, lucerne 150 kg N/ha; root N may add about 30% ([SA Soil Quality](https://www.soilquality.org.au/factsheets/legumes-and-nitrogen-fixation-south-australia)).
- **Another figure, a little higher:** NSW Government guidance gives roughly 25 kg N per tonne DM for field pea, vetch or faba, so a late-winter sowing making about 3 t/ha DM could fix about 75 kg N/ha. It is broadacre, so garden yields will be lower and this is an upper bound [inference] ([NSW Government](https://www.nsw.gov.au/regional-and-primary-industries/agriculture/cropping/cover-cropping)). The two sources differ (about 20 versus 25 kg N/t); neither is a garden measurement.
- **Harvesting removes it.** 30-40% of crop N sits in the seed and leaves with the harvest, so a green manure returns far more N than a bean crop you pick. Fixation falls when soil mineral N exceeds about 35 kg/ha; a healthy field pea crop can fix up to about 200 kg N/ha ([SA Soil Quality](https://www.soilquality.org.au/factsheets/legumes-and-nitrogen-fixation-south-australia)).
- **The benefit is brief.** In NSW grain systems, wheat after pulses yielded 0.5-1.5 t/ha more, matching 40-100 kg fertiliser N/ha. Part of it (0.2-0.5 t/ha) was a disease break. In five of six sites a second wheat crop showed no effect. Faba bean and lupin are classed as strong fixers; field pea, chickpea and lentil as medium. A one-year annual medic ley raised sowing soil nitrate by 74-88 kg N/ha ([GRDC 2009](https://grdc.com.au/Research-and-Development/GRDC-Update-Papers/2009/09/NITROGEN-FIXATION-BENEFITS-OF-PULSE-CROPS)). Evidence: strong for the trend, broadacre for the numbers.
- **Terminating field pea around the end of October** maximised dry matter and N in southern NSW. N benefit falls if grain is harvested or if disease, weeds, drought or late sowing cut growth. Green and brown manuring usually returned negative first-year margins in broadacre ([GRDC southern NSW pulses](https://grdc.com.au/Research-and-Development/GRDC-Update-Papers/2013/07/The-role-of-pulses-and-their-management-in-southern-NSW)).
- **Wet tropics (Queensland cane fallows):** one study found covers gave 5-10 t/ha biomass and most legumes fixed over 50 kg N/ha, with weeds suppressed. Mixed species lowered the risk of a failed season. No cover crop raised mycorrhizal colonisation or reduced pathogenic nematodes ([Soil CRC](https://soilcrc.com.au/?p=6583)). A different cane-industry guide (Tully) estimates fresh biomass of 8-32 t/ha and total N (including roots) of 90-360 kg/ha for soybean, 70-290 for cowpea and 60-240 for lablab. Its own 2019-22 samples ranged about 15-225 kg/ha total N, highest for sunn hemp and well-grown soybean. Fallows run 3-4 months and sowing months were not given ([SRA Tully guide](https://sugarresearch.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Tully-Fallow-Crops-Guide_2023.pdf)). The two sources give different ranges, and both are commercial cane systems.
- **Southern summer covers disappoint.** In SA, Vic and NSW, summer covers usually gave under 2 t/ha, did not reliably raise soil carbon over 3-4 years, were hard to establish in dry conditions and used subsoil water; cowpea and mixes rooted deeper and dried the subsoil more ([Soil CRC](https://soilcrc.com.au/?p=6583)). In central-west NSW they used 30-50 mm of soil water and reduced the following wheat yield; cowpea gave little benefit and millet established poorly ([GRDC 2020](https://grdc.com.au/resources-and-publications/grdc-update-papers/tab-content/grdc-update-papers/2020/02/summer-cover-crops-in-short-fallow-do-they-have-a-place-in-central-nsw)). Home gardeners in dry temperate zones should prefer winter-growing legumes and irrigate summer covers only if water allows [inference].
- **Inoculation and nodule checks:** inoculate if the legume has not been grown in the paddock or bed in the last four years or the right strain has not been used. Survival and nodulation drop below pH 5, except narrow-leaf lupin ([SA Soil Quality](https://www.soilquality.org.au/factsheets/legumes-and-nitrogen-fixation-south-australia)). Check nodules at 4-6 weeks: pink or red inside means fixing. Match the inoculant group: cowpea and mung bean group I; lablab and pigeon pea J; sunn hemp and velvet bean M; soybean H ([Sugar Research Australia](https://sugarresearch.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Checklist-for-Growing-Legume-Cover-Crops-in-the-Wet-Tropics_D.04.pdf)). The Herbert fallow guide gives the same groups for cowpea, lablab, soybean and sunn hemp ([SRA Herbert guide](https://sugarresearch.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Growing-Legume-Fallow-Crops_2018_D.01.pdf)).
- **When to terminate:** cut or dig in at the start of flowering. After full flowering and seed set, N falls and plants can self-seed. Wait 4-6 weeks before sowing, or leave the cut cover as surface mulch ([SGA green manure](https://sgaonline.org.au/green-manure)). Diggers suggests 3-4 weeks in warm soil and a week or two more in cool months ([Diggers](https://www.diggers.com.au/blogs/learn/green-manures-q-a); commercial source). For cane fallows, delay incorporation 2-4 weeks after crimping or slashing to retain N, and terminate before seed set ([SRA Herbert guide](https://sugarresearch.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Growing-Legume-Fallow-Crops_2018_D.01.pdf)).
- **Soil pH and the legume:** rhizobia struggle in acid soil, and liming helps ([GRDC 2022](https://grdc.com.au/resources-and-publications/grdc-update-papers/tab-content/grdc-update-papers/2022/02/removing-yield-barriers-caused-by-acidity-and-preventing-subsoil-acidification)). Matching acid sands to lupin or serradella and alkaline soils to medic, vetch, field pea or faba is [general knowledge]; confirm with your state agriculture department.

## Non-legume cover crops and residue

- **C:N matters.** Microbes work best around C:N 24:1; above about 30:1 decomposition slows and N can be drawn from the soil. Reported C:N: oat straw 70:1, cereal rye straw 82:1, cereal/mustard mix 45:1, forage radish and mustards 15:1, tillage radish 14:1, green sunn hemp 10:1 (breaks down quickly) ([AUSVEG/SoilWealth](https://ausveg.com.au/app/uploads/2024/09/ManagingCoverCropResiduesSOILWEALTHwebversion.pdf)). Practical consequence [inference from C:N data]: after a cut grass or cereal cover, allow 3-4 weeks or use transplants.
- **Termination timing:** early termination speeds breakdown, reduces phytotoxicity and prevents seed set; late termination gives more biomass, mulch and weed suppression. The factsheet does not address volunteer or regrowth weed risk ([AUSVEG/SoilWealth](https://ausveg.com.au/app/uploads/2024/09/ManagingCoverCropResiduesSOILWEALTHwebversion.pdf)).
- **Termination methods (farm scale):** non-selective herbicide is the quick, cheap option and often the only route to a full kill of cereals and grasses (follow the product label or permit). Flail mowing is even but slower and costlier. Roller-crimping works on tall species and leaves mulch, but kill depends on stage: cereal rye went from 21% to 93% kill as it matured. Incorporation speeds breakdown ([AUSVEG/SoilWealth](https://ausveg.com.au/app/uploads/2024/09/ManagingCoverCropResiduesSOILWEALTHwebversion.pdf)). For a no-herbicide garden, slash or chop before seed set and leave as mulch [inference]; frost-killing or smothering under cardboard or tarp is plausible but not sourced.
- **Cereal rye allelopathy** is shown in controlled settings, but field evidence is weak (Wisconsin data). Early stunting of the next crop is more likely N tie-up. Rolling rye needs full flowering, which risks seed set if left too long ([UW IPCM](https://ipcm.wisc.edu/blog/2022/05/cereal-rye-cover-crop-for-weed-suppression)).
- **Biofumigation is inconsistent.** In one Gatton (Qld) trial, one site, heavy clay, one cycle, *Brassica juncea* killed 67-93% of buried *Sclerotium rolfsii* sclerotia in late winter and spring, and Nemat rocket and tillage radish reached 93-100% against Macrophomina in late winter and spring. For Sclerotinia, plain fallow was as good or better. Some varieties needed repeated insecticides for aphids and Rutherglen bug, and two canola-type lines failed to flower. Nematodes were not tested ([Duff & Firrell 2021](https://era.dpi.qld.gov.au/id/eprint/8750/1/J.-Duff-and-M.-Firrell-2021-Biofumigation--A-Cover-Crop-Option-12-Months-of-the-Year-to-Manage-Three-Soilborne-Pathogens-Ailing-the-Australian-Vegetable-Industry.pdf)). Evidence: moderate and conditional. Biofumigant brassicas can also raise clubroot risk (see above).
- **Nematode-resistant covers (glasshouse only).** Queensland glasshouse screening found sunn hemp resisted *M. enterolobii*, *M. incognita* and *M. javanica*; forage sorghum 'Jumbo' and oat 'Swan' were highly resistant to all three; most soybean varieties and many biofumigants were susceptible; a mixture works only if every component is resistant ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/knowledge-hub/cover-crops-to-reduce-root-knot-nematode-populations/)). **Conflict to check:** NT DAF says guava root-knot nematode (GRKN) infects sunn hemp and advises avoiding it where GRKN is present ([NT DAF](https://daf.nt.gov.au/news/2026/root-knot-nematodes-in-the-nt)). If GRKN is the species *M. enterolobii* [general knowledge], the two sources disagree. In the Top End, ask NT DAF before using sunn hemp.
- **Marigolds as a nematode crop:** see the tier table below. Alice Springs sources list French marigold as a nematode-resistant green manure (practitioner source, no trial data) ([Alice Springs guide](https://assets.nationbuilder.com/aridlands/pages/4419/attachments/original/1726025047/Vegie_companion_small2.pdf)).

## Soil problems and the sourced fixes

Test first. Gypsum for sodic clay and lime for acid soil should follow a soil test, not be applied by default, and acidity is a different problem from sodicity [inference].

- **Acidity.** Acidification comes from ammonium fertiliser, nitrate leaching and removal of plant material; treat early because subsoil acidity is hard to fix ([Qld Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/management/soil/degradation/acidification)). Two GRDC papers give slightly different broadacre targets: pHCa above 5.5 ([GRDC 2022](https://grdc.com.au/resources-and-publications/grdc-update-papers/tab-content/grdc-update-papers/2022/08/soil-acidity-its-stratification-and-amelioration-with-lime)), and 5.5-6.0, where CaCl2 reads about 0.7 below water pH. Lime moves only about 1-2 cm a year, and lime above about 2 t/ha on sand, 3 t/ha on sandy loam or 4 t/ha on loam risks micronutrient deficiency, so split applications ([GRDC 2022b](https://grdc.com.au/resources-and-publications/grdc-update-papers/tab-content/grdc-update-papers/2022/02/removing-yield-barriers-caused-by-acidity-and-preventing-subsoil-acidification)). These are broadacre figures. Brassica vegetables prefer higher pH (clubroot advice: 7.0-7.5). A TAFE Queensland source aims for pH about 6-6.5 for summer vegetables in south-east Queensland ([TAFE Qld](https://tafeqld.edu.au/news-and-events/news/2018/growing-summer-vegetables)). The sources reviewed give no garden lime rates.
- **Sodic, dispersive clay (Sodosols).** Gypsum reduces sodicity and dispersion, and the rate comes from a lab test ([CCMA](https://soilhealth.ccmaknowledgebase.vic.gov.au/SoilHealthResource/documents/outputs/education_and_training/Dispersive%20Soils%20-%20Agriculture%20Note.pdf)). Also raise surface organic matter, reduce cultivation, work soil at the right moisture, and use raised beds and drainage ([Agriculture Victoria](https://vro.agriculture.vic.gov.au/DPI/Vro/vrosite.nsf/pages/hort_vic_soils-sodosol)). A WA gypsum table runs from 0-1.5 t/ha at slight dispersion on neutral or acid soils up to 5 t/ha for severe; it is broadacre guidance from southern WA, so apply with caution to gardens ([DPIRD WA](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/node/5152)).
- **Water-repellent sand.** High risk across 3.3 million ha of south-west WA and moderate on a further 6.9 million ha ([DPIRD](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/water-repellence/soil-water-repellence-overview?page=0%2C0)). Broadacre banded wetting agents gave 0-20% yield responses, were negative where the product had no water-retention chemistry, and were more reliable on forest gravels than deep sands; the page may be out of date ([DPIRD wetting agents](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/node/280)). For Perth gardens, the Water Corporation advises wetting agent at planting and again at the start of winter, early spring and early summer; organic matter into the top 30 cm in late autumn; 5-10 cm of mulch (which can save up to 20% of garden water); and pH about 6.0-7.0 ([Water Corporation](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/save-water/in-the-garden/improving-your-soil)). A school guide suggests bentonite at 6-8 kg/m2 dug into the top 25 cm; the sources reviewed flag that rate as looking high and not cross-checked. Claying and soil inversion are machinery-scale practices ([DPIRD](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/water-repellence/soil-water-repellence-overview?page=0%2C0)).
- **Arid irrigation and salt (Alice Springs sources).** Mulch 7-10 cm deep and off stems; drip is the most efficient; irrigation raises soil pH over time, so use compost and sulphur and avoid lime; salts build near the surface, and heavy mulch plus a deep watering helps. The guide claims compost may cut nematode populations by up to 90% (its claim, not a trial) ([Alice Springs guide](https://assets.nationbuilder.com/aridlands/pages/4419/attachments/original/1726025047/Vegie_companion_small2.pdf)).
- **No-dig.** SGA's fact sheet describes layers of cardboard, green and brown materials, aged manure and compost, with at least 5 hours of sun. It cites no studies, so evidence is weak-anecdotal. No Australian source compares no-dig with tillage ([SGA No-Dig](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/no-dig.pdf)).

## Companion planting: evidence versus folklore

A narrative review finds efficacy mixed and mechanisms poorly understood, with many "repellent" claims resting on lab work or anecdote ([Parker et al. 2013](https://core.ac.uk/download/322420712.pdf)). SGA reports limited scientific information overall. Most evidence is from overseas; the Australian trials found are for brassicas.

| Tier | Practice | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Supported** (moderate) | Flowering insectary strips | Australian field trials: 1 m strips beside brassicas lifted beneficials, still elevated at least 15 m into the crop. Diamondback moth parasitism more than doubled; caterpillar suppression was only a trend because pest density was very low. Alyssum flowers longest; cornflower is best for parasitoid wasps; buckwheat is fast but short-flowering, frost tender and least good for parasitoids; coriander was not tested ([Hort Innovation VG16062](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg16062-boosting-beneficials-in-your-vegetable-crop.pdf)) |
| **Supported, with caveats** | Trap crops | Mixed trap species beat single species for flea beetle. Trap crops only work if more attractive than the crop for long enough, and need pests removed ([Parker et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/322420712.pdf); [UMN Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/planting-and-growing-guides/companion-planting-home-gardens)). Yellow rocket was hard to establish from seed in drought and worked better with irrigation or transplants ([VG16062](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg16062-boosting-beneficials-in-your-vegetable-crop.pdf)) |
| **Supported, as rotation not interplanting** (moderate) | French or African marigold (Tagetes) against root-knot nematode | California field trials: *T. patula* 'Single Gold' grown as a full-area prior crop reduced RKN on following tomato. Tomato yield was about 50% higher than after fallow, and melon yield +95%. At one site it matched methyl iodide fumigation; at the other fumigation was better. Cultivar-specific and non-Australian; one abstract read ([PubMed 30818673](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30818673/)). Scattered plants are unlikely to work; it needs dense planting dug in over a season ([Happy Valley Seeds](https://www.happyvalleyseeds.com.au/blogs/companion-planting/companion-planting-australia)). Cultivars effective against the nematodes present in Australia were not sourced |
| **Weak or mixed** | Alliums against pests; marigold or mint against insects; nasturtium as trap; sage or thyme against diamondback moth | Marigold and mint did not repel onion or cabbage root fly. Marigold against Colorado potato beetle is contradicted by multiple studies. Nasturtium is "a pretty aphid nursery" unless pests are removed. Sage and thyme with Brussels sprouts had no effect on diamondback moth in one study ([Parker et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/322420712.pdf); [UMN](https://extension.umn.edu/planting-and-growing-guides/companion-planting-home-gardens); [Happy Valley](https://www.happyvalleyseeds.com.au/blogs/companion-planting/companion-planting-australia)) |
| **Plausible, thin** | Three Sisters (corn, beans, squash) | Long cultural history, but benefits are not tied to studies in the sources read. Legume N mainly helps the following crop, not neighbours; climbing beans on corn is mainly physical support ([Happy Valley](https://www.happyvalleyseeds.com.au/blogs/companion-planting/companion-planting-australia)). Oats between broad bean rows for chocolate spot is a practitioner claim |
| **Folklore / unsupported** | Basil improving tomato flavour or yield; carrot-tomato pairing | Three-year blind taste tests found no difference. The carrot-tomato claim traced to a 1975 book title ([Laidback Gardener](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2026/06/04/not-another-article-on-companion-planting-or-what-science-has-to-say-about-it/?amp=1), secondary source; [Happy Valley](https://www.happyvalleyseeds.com.au/blogs/companion-planting/companion-planting-australia)). Basil and marigold did reduce thrips on tomato in some studies ([UMN](https://extension.umn.edu/planting-and-growing-guides/companion-planting-home-gardens)), so basil is fine for convenience, not for flavour |
| **Folklore** | The NT Government companion page (basil with tomato, nasturtium under fruit trees, marigolds "keep soil free of nematodes", and bad pairs such as fennel and tomato) | The page cites no studies and does not mark any claim as anecdotal ([NT Government](https://nt.gov.au/environment/home-gardens/companion-planting)). Treat all of it as uncited practice |
| **Folklore, orchard "accumulators"** | Comfrey as a nutrient accumulator, daffodil pest repellence | Guild sources cite no evidence ([djplaner guild page](https://djplaner.github.io/memex/sense/landscape-garden/tree-guilds.html); [Diggers](https://www.diggers.com.au/blogs/learn/underplanting-orchards)) |

- **Overseas supported cases** (review, not Australian): alfalfa trap crop for Lygus in cotton; early potato trap crops for Colorado potato beetle; mixed mustard, pak choi and rape trap crops for flea beetle in broccoli; push-pull in Kenyan maize; dill with eggplant reducing Colorado potato beetle through predators; phacelia raising syrphid density near cabbage ([Parker et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/322420712.pdf)).
- **Fennel as an antagonist:** fennel roots are said to slow tomato, beans and legumes. The only source is secondary, so isolating fennel is a reasonable precaution with thin evidence. Allium-with-legume antagonism: no source found, so treat it as folklore ([Laidback Gardener](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2026/06/04/not-another-article-on-companion-planting-or-what-science-has-to-say-about-it/?amp=1)).
- **Chinese chives with tomato** reduced bacterial wilt in a 1999 study, mainly a tropical disease. This is a blog summary and the study was not verified at source ([Laidback Gardener](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2026/06/04/not-another-article-on-companion-planting-or-what-science-has-to-say-about-it/?amp=1)).
- **Orchard understorey (Victoria, 2001-03):** results were mixed. Buckwheat and chicory/yarrow were marginally best; mustard raised russet and mite numbers ([Hort Innovation AP00033](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/orchard-habitat-management-to-enhance-ipm-systems-in-pome-fruit-ap00033.pdf)).
- **Pests and sprays:** see [insect pests](pests/insects.md) and [diseases, weeds and soil problems](pests/diseases.md).

## Gaps

- **Garden-scale rates.** Seeding rates, gypsum and lime rates, claying and N-fixation figures are broadacre, cane or vineyard values. Conversions to garden beds are not sourced.
- **Tropical and arid covers.** No Top End biomass or N data for cowpea, lablab or mung bean; no sowing months for cane fallows; Darwin sowing months are not sourced. Arid-zone legume and grain performance is not sourced beyond Alice Springs.
- **Rotation sources.** No NSW DPI, Agriculture Victoria or Queensland DAF rotation page was read. No source for white rot (Allium). The VG15010 break lengths are commercial figures and the excerpt read stopped before the cucumber-family chapter. Rocket, basil, kang kong, lemon grass and sweet potato have no rotation source beyond what is noted above.
- **Companions.** No Australian trials outside brassicas; no tropical companion trials; three sisters, alliums with carrot fly, brassica with strawberry and similar pairs are unsourced.
- **Soil.** Compost and mulch evidence for home gardens, biochar, mycorrhizae, worm farms, salinity thresholds and soil-test interpretation are not sourced. No weed-risk data for specific covers (lablab, sunn hemp seed set); see [Biosecurity and weeds](biosecurity-and-weeds.md).
- **Regions.** Tasmania and Victoria have almost no sourced soil-building detail. Check Agriculture Victoria, NRE Tasmania and your state agriculture department.
