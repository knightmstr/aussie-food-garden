# New South Wales and the ACT

> **How reliable is this page?** Canberra and the ACT are the best covered: a government (ACT Government) note, Bureau of Meteorology frost statements and several newspaper and community calendars. Sydney now has a month-by-month table from the City of Ryde seed library (one council, one seed list). The Northern Rivers (a proxy from south-east Queensland only), the tablelands and inland NSW have no readable month-by-month calendar, so those sections say so and point to the best timing advice available. Most NSW fruit, nut, cover-crop and native-food facts come from NSW Government pages, but several are broadacre or industry figures, and no NSW weed list, ACT biosecurity rules or fire ant carrier list was read. Treat every date as a guide and check it against your own frost pattern.

## Soil, cover crops and rotation

**Cover crops (NSW Government).**
- A cover crop is sown for groundcover and usually terminated before seed set. Benefits listed: weed suppression, short-term soil carbon gain, more microbial biomass and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, faster breakdown of disease-carrying residues. Trade-offs: cost and possible soil-moisture draw-down for the next crop (broadacre, partly garbled transcript) [100](../references.md#r100).
- Legumes such as field pea, vetch or faba bean fix roughly 25 kg N per tonne of dry matter; a late-winter sowing making about 3 t/ha could fix about 75 kg N/ha (same source). [inference] This is a broadacre figure; garden yields will be lower, so treat it as an upper bound.
- Mix grasses, broadleaves and legumes. Buckwheat is cited for phosphorus scavenging and phacelia for attracting bees. Younger covers break down faster and release N sooner; mature ones are slower and may tie up N (same source).
- Southern NSW pulses (GRDC): N fixed tracks dry matter; terminating field pea around the end of October maximises dry matter and N; the benefit falls if grain is harvested or growth is cut by disease, weeds, drought or late sowing (2013, field pea focus, broadacre) [225](../references.md#r225).
- Cool-winter areas including southern NSW: sow green manures in early autumn (or after autumn rain if dry); early spring also works. Warm-season species go in after frost where it is cold. Cut or dig in at the start of flowering, then wait 4-6 weeks before sowing, or leave as surface mulch (practitioner) [270](../references.md#r270).
- Cool-season species: broad (fava) beans, tick beans, fenugreek, lupins, oats, subclover, woolly pod vetch, rye, mustards, wheat, barley. Warm-season: buckwheat, cowpea, millets, lablab, marigolds, mung bean, soybean (same source).

**NSW trial results.**
- Vineyard mid-row crops (NSW): field pea gave the highest dry matter and N fixation; faba bean gave good biomass and strongly attracted bees; lupins failed because they were sown after the recommended early April. The seeding rates (field pea 100, faba 180, crimson clover 8-10 kg/ha) are vineyard rates (government-hosted fact sheet) [77](../references.md#r77). [inference] Gardeners usually sow denser; the garden conversion is unverified.
- Summer covers in central-west NSW used 30-50 mm of soil water, reduced the following wheat yield, cowpea gave little benefit to the next crop and millet established poorly (broadacre) [226](../references.md#r226). In dry southern conditions summer covers are only tactically useful[201](../references.md#r201).
- Biofumigant brassicas give inconsistent results and are risky in clubroot beds (see Crop guide).

**Sample rotation (SGA).** The four-bed, four-season cycle is Legume, then Heavy feeder, then Light feeder, then Green manure, with beds staggered; no same-family crops back to back, no heavy feeder after a heavy feeder, a green manure at least once every four seasons (practitioner; the page has internal inconsistencies, for example lettuce is listed as both daisy family and heavy feeder) [267](../references.md#r267).
- [inference] Temperate adaptation: Bed 1 autumn broad bean or pea; Bed 2 tomato or pumpkin the next spring; Bed 3 carrot, beetroot or onion; Bed 4 oats or buckwheat cut before seed set. Brassicas get their own long, clubroot-aware cycle.
- Tomato and potato share blight, so keep them apart in the rotation (commercial) [312](../references.md#r312).

**Soil problems.**
- Acidity: Australian soils tend to be acidic; one lime application can last two to three years; too much lime can harm; test pH periodically (the page did not mention Queanbeyan or Canberra, so this is general advice) [333](../references.md#r333). GRDC now recommends keeping pHCa above 5.5 and sampling in 5 cm increments[229](../references.md#r229). A second GRDC paper gives 5.5-6.0 and warns that lime above about 2 t/ha on sand risks micronutrient deficiency[228](../references.md#r228). The two give different targets and both are broadacre.
- Clay warms slowly, drains poorly and is sticky wet and hard dry; compost and coarse sand can make a productive loam (Queanbeyan Age, above). Gypsum helps sodic, dispersive clays only, and the rate comes from a lab test[357](../references.md#r357). For sodosols: gypsum, surface organic matter, raised beds and drainage (Victorian government) [11](../references.md#r11).
- The no-dig method (layers of cardboard, green and brown materials, aged manure, compost) is practitioner advice with no studies cited (evidence: weak/anecdotal) [271](../references.md#r271). The ACT Government describes a no-dig verge garden method (overlapping newspaper, compost, mulch) [2](../references.md#r2).
- No source was found on Sydney's Wianamatta shale clay, sandy coastal soils, Southern Tablelands granitic soils, or acid-soil management specific to NSW.

## Pests and biosecurity here

See also [insect pests](../pests/insects.md), [diseases, weeds and soil problems](../pests/diseases.md) and [biosecurity and weeds](../biosecurity-and-weeds.md).

> **Read first:** use any bait, spray or treatment exactly as its label or permit says. The NSW notes name no Qfly bait product and give no bee-safety advice, so check the product label before using anything near flowering plants or hives.

**Queensland fruit fly (Qfly).**
- Bait refuge trees and sheltered areas from late July to August to cut overwintering survivors, and keep baiting through autumn and early winter. Empty winter traps do not mean the flies are gone (government) [92](../references.md#r92).
- In winter Qfly shelters in avocado, citrus, loquat, native vegetation, olive or avocado windbreaks, and lemon trees near sheds or houses (same source).
- The Greater Sunraysia Pest Free Area (along the Murray from Kerang to Wentworth, and the Darling to Pooncarie) allows marketing without postharvest treatment. Host produce moving within NSW and Victoria outside it needs no treatment but must be free of Qfly. Report or enquire on 1800 680 244. The page did not state current NSW outbreak or exclusion zone details (same source).
- Sanitation (SGA, 2007, anecdotal, no trials): pick ripe fruit before it drops; bag fallen fruit and leave it sealed in the sun for 3-7 days; use pheromone traps; chickens eat fruit and pupae; newly emerged adults sit on the ground for up to 24 hours; Clivia is a host[273](../references.md#r273).

**Red imported fire ants.** No NSW detections since July 2025. Earlier nests at South Murwillumbah (November 2023), Wardell (January 2024) and Tweed Heads (July 2025) were destroyed and surveillance continues. Moving defined carrier materials from the red, orange and yellow zones is controlled under the Biosecurity (Fire Ant) Emergency Order. Report through the online form or 1800 680 244. The carrier list was not on the page read, so check it before moving soil, mulch or plants (government) [86](../references.md#r86).

**Varroa mite (beekeepers).** Eradication was judged not technically feasible on 19 September 2023, so NSW moved to management. Kempsey, the Hunter and the Central Coast are the Management (orange) Zone; the rest of NSW is the Suppression (green) Zone. All beekeepers must register and test every 16 weeks (alcohol wash, soapy water, sugar shake or miticide strip). Treat all hives within 14 days if a test finds more than 5 mites (March-November) or more than 9 (December-February); keep records for 5 years. Hive movements from the Management Zone need surveillance within 14 days and a declaration. Swarm capture is allowed if you are registered. The wild honey bee baiting program has finished. The page did not quantify the pollination impact (government) [97](../references.md#r97). [inference] A home beekeeper in Sydney outside the Central Coast is in the Suppression Zone but still subject to registration and testing.

**Myrtle rust.** Infects Myrtaceae (over 300 hosts recorded), including eucalypts, bottlebrush, paperbark, tea tree and lilly pilly. It starts as purple spots on soft new growth, then bright yellow pustules. Check plants before buying, do not move infected plants, wash clothing used in high-risk areas, rotate fungicides, and remove infected plants by bagging or solarising for 3-4 weeks; do not use green waste bins. Lemon myrtle is not named on the DPI page and no reporting information was given (government) [90](../references.md#r90). [inference] Lemon myrtle, riberry and lilly pilly are all Myrtaceae and so should be treated as susceptible; the lemon myrtle link is not verified on a fetched page.

**Weeds.** No NSW prohibited or priority weed list was read, so no garden or cover plant can be classed as declared here. Check the NSW Government weed lists and your local council before planting anything that spreads (these were not read for this page).
