## Soil, cover crops and rotation

### Perth sand and water repellence

- Sandy topsoils under 5% clay are most susceptible to water repellence. In south-west WA about 3.3 million ha are high risk and 6.9 million ha moderate risk. Dry autumns, dry sowing and narrow points make it worse; perennial shrubs, pastures and trees are less affected once established. Signs: dry patches between moist depressions, patchy or delayed emergence, runoff. DPIRD overview[66](../references.md#r66) (government, broadacre, page may be out of date)
- Fixes in that overview: claying lasts up to 30 years and works best in wetter areas; soil inversion buries repellent topsoil for 7+ years; rotary spading lasts 3 to 5 years; furrow sowing, sowing on old rows, delaying sowing until rain and banded wetters help. These are machinery-scale practices. Transferable ideas for a home garden: avoid dry-sowing, water in by hand until wet, keep perennial or mulched cover on sand. [inference]
- Claying detail: spread subsoil should be roughly 35 to 40% clay; up to 150 t/ha in deep sand was cost-effective in medium-to-low rainfall WA trials; poor incorporation or excess clay gave little or negative yield effect in short dry seasons. A droplet taking more than 10 seconds to soak in is repellent; above 60 seconds may need management. GRDC Groundcover[367](../references.md#r367)
- Wetting agents (DPIRD, broadacre): banded wetters gave 0 to 20% crop yield responses and were negative with no water-retention chemistry; more reliable on south-west forest gravel soils than deep sands; some last up to two seasons. The effect is real but inconsistent, so pair a wetting agent with organic matter and mulch rather than relying on it alone. DPIRD wetting agents[65](../references.md#r65) (page under review); [inference]
- Home advice for Perth sand (Water Corporation): apply a granular or liquid wetting agent at planting and reapply at the start of winter, early spring and early summer, watering in. Work organic matter into the top 30 cm in late autumn before planting. Mulch 5 to 10 cm (straw or lucerne for vegetable beds), which can save up to 20% of garden water. Aim for pH about 6.0 to 7.0. Water Corporation[171](../references.md#r171) (government)
- The school guide suggests bentonite about 6 to 8 kg/m2 for very sandy soil, dug into the top 25 cm (surface scatter causes caking), plus zeolite and rock dust. This is a single non-specialist source and the bentonite rate looks high and was not cross-checked. Waste Authority[170](../references.md#r170)
- An open question: a retrieved DPIRD source treats surface organic matter as the cause of repellence while home advice recommends compost and mulch. The sources reviewed did not reconcile them. DPIRD[66](../references.md#r66)

### Acid sands, soil-building legumes and rotation

- Lupins suit acidic sandy WA soils, fix nitrogen and break cereal disease cycles. Serradella suits light acidic soils, where phosphorus and sulphur are often lacking, and responds to lime at 1 to 2 t/ha on extremely acidic soils. DPIRD lupins[54](../references.md#r54); Serradella Part D[62](../references.md#r62) (the guide cites NSW trial sites, so its numbers are not WA-specific)
- SerraMax (bred by DPIRD from seed collected on Santorini) suits acidic sands in low to medium rainfall, is hard-seeded and regenerates well. It is a farm pasture release, with no garden seeding rates, so garden use is untested. WA Government, Dec 2021[169](../references.md#r169); [inference]
- Legume pastures on low-fertility soils fix about 15 to 20 kg N per tonne of dry matter and a well-managed pasture adds about 40 kg N/ha/year, up to 100 or more in good seasons. The source cites NSW sites, so treat as indicative. Serradella Part D[62](../references.md#r62)
- Matching soil to legume (acid sands to lupin and serradella; alkaline soils to medic, vetch, field pea, faba) is [general knowledge]. Perth's limestone ridges are alkaline, so the pairing differs within the city. Confirm locally.
- Garden-scale seeding rates for these legumes were not sourced for WA. Vineyard trials in NSW used field pea 100 kg/ha and faba bean 180 kg/ha, converted to about 10 and 18 g/m2, but home gardeners usually sow denser. DPIRD-hosted vineyard fact sheet[77](../references.md#r77) (non-WA); [inference]
- Summer cover crops used 30 to 50 mm of soil water in a NSW trial, which matters in Perth gardens: spring-sown green manures need irrigation. GRDC[226](../references.md#r226) (non-WA, broadacre); [inference]

> **Sample Perth garden rotation [inference, not tested in WA].** Autumn: faba or field pea, or oats, as a cover on a resting bed (sown by mid-autumn, sown dry only where you can water). Spring: tomato or cucurbits after dig-in plus four to six weeks. Summer: only irrigated or heavily mulched crops. For the family and feeder rules behind a four-bed cycle (legume, then heavy feeder, then light feeder, then green manure), see [Principles](../principles.md).

### Other WA soil problems

- **Alkaline and limestone sands:** lime-induced iron chlorosis shows as yellow-white leaves, notably on limestone coastal sands. DPIRD's remedy: confirm with an iron chelate foliar spray (leaves green within a week), then mix iron sulphate into compost (1 cup per 10 L of moistened compost, packed into holes about 20 cm deep around the root zone). Go slowly: a pH unit is a tenfold change. Moderate; page possibly out of date. DPIRD soil pH[64](../references.md#r64)
- **Salty bore water (home gardens):** leaf burn is the main symptom; test bore water repeatedly after running the bore 10 minutes; on clay, gypsum (up to 5 kg per square metre) helps flush salt; blend or alternate bore and scheme water; avoid muriate of potash. Page last updated 2014 and under review. DPIRD minimising salt damage[58](../references.md#r58)
- **Sodic clays (Wheatbelt, broadacre):** gypsum t/ha by dispersion level: slight (ESP 6 to 10) 0 to 1.5 on neutral or acid soils and 1.0 to 2.5 on alkaline; moderate (ESP 10 to 15) 2.5 and 5.0; severe (over 15) 5.0. Rates above 5 t/ha gave no extra yield in the first five years in WA trials. Lime does nothing on alkaline soils, and ripping can bring up salt and boron. Apply with caution to gardens. DPIRD dispersive soils[55](../references.md#r55)
- **Kimberley soils:** trial soils ranged from pindan red sand (about 90% sand, 0.32% carbon at Broome) to cracking black clay at Kununurra (0.43% carbon). Legumes trialled there (lablab, cowpea, siratro, butterfly pea, stylos, leucaena) were forage, not human food. DPIRD[78](../references.md#r78) (possibly out of date)
- **Orchard floor (SA, non-WA):** medic plus ryegrass raised nitrogen fixation and soil carbon in Riverland and Barossa trials; tall fescue in the tree row raised carbon but competes for water. SA Drought Hub[199](../references.md#r199)

## Pests and biosecurity here

See [insect pests](../pests/insects.md), [diseases, weeds and soil problems](../pests/diseases.md) and [Biosecurity and weeds](../biosecurity-and-weeds.md).

> **Read first:** use any product exactly as its label or permit says. Spray bait late in the day and avoid flowering plants to protect bees. Maldison has a 4-day wait before picking fruit (per DPIRD); check the label for any product.

### Medfly (Mediterranean fruit fly)

- **Status:** a declared pest and prohibited organism under section 12 of the Biosecurity and Agriculture Management Act 2007. Anyone finding or suspecting it must report to DPIRD. It is endemic to WA, found from Esperance to Carnarvon, with the main infestation from Perth to Bunbury, Bridgetown, Donnybrook and Manjimup. Control is described as mandatory in Carnarvon, Armadale, Kalamunda, Mundaring, Serpentine-Jarrahdale and Swan (a summarised page: check the wording on DPIRD's site). It is present in Broome and Derby but absent from the Ord River Irrigation Area (Kununurra). DPIRD Medfly[56](../references.md#r56) (government)
- **Backyard control, DPIRD "backyard management" sheet:** start in spring (adults active above 12 C). Sanitation first: remove fallen and unwanted fruit, including ripe fruit left on the tree, and treat before disposal (boil or freeze two days, solarise in a sealed black bag 3 to 5 days, soak in water with an oil film 3 days, or bury at least 1 m deep). Protein bait with spinosad or maldison as 60 to 100 mL spot sprays per tree, reapplied weekly or after more than 5 mm of rain (maldison: do not pick fruit for 4 days). Hang traps about 1.5 m high in shaded canopy and along boundaries, at least two per tree. Ceratrap is the only registered attract-and-kill device, according to this sheet. Netting, bags and pruning help, and community baiting with neighbours helps. Report via MyPestGuide Reporter. DPIRD Medfly backyard management[45](../references.md#r45)
- **A second DPIRD page differs.** Baiting is to start when fruit is about one-third final size, with coarse droplets at least 2 mm, reapplied weekly and after more than 5 mm rain; spinosad has a same-day harvest and maldison a 4-day wait. Disposal: freeze at least 24 hours, or soak under an oil film for 7 or more days. Traps: at least two home-made traps per tree, 1.5 to 2 m high, in shade, no more than 5 to 6 m apart. Netting: mosquito netting, shade cloth or flyscreen on a frame, only while fruit ripens; no mesh size is given. This page is flagged as possibly out of date. Where the two disagree on times, follow the current DPIRD page and the product label. DPIRD backyard control[57](../references.md#r57)
- **Evidence:** exclusion (netting, bags) and sanitation are strong (Queensland fruit fly work, applied to Medfly [inference]); baiting is moderate; home-made lure recipes are moderate for monitoring and weak for suppression; no single method is complete. Spinosad bait is the organic-relevant option, applied as bait, not on flowering plants.
- **Interstate:** do not carry fruit or fruiting vegetables from WA or SA into Victoria. Agriculture Victoria[9](../references.md#r9)
