# South Australia

> **How reliable is this page?** The Adelaide plains calendar is a government source (Green Adelaide), and fruit fly rules, Riverland irrigation history, orchard-floor trials, quandong and declared weeds are well sourced. There is no Hills calendar, no Fleurieu, Kangaroo Island or McLaren Vale food-crop data, no far-north vegetable calendar and almost no SA-authored fruit and nut care pages. Several soil sources are broadacre trials (Eyre Peninsula) or from other states, and are labelled.

### Adelaide Hills, Barossa and other regions

No Hills calendar or last-frost date by suburb was found. The Hills usually have more rain and cooler nights than the plains [general knowledge], so expect warm-season crops to go in later and frost to matter at higher sites [inference]. Use the BoM statement that frost risk typically ends by November as an outer bound for tender crops, not as a planting date[21](../references.md#r21).

For the Riverland, Eyre, Yorke, mid-north and far north there is no calendar. The best sourced timing advice is for field crops: sow field pea in May, sow faba in May (earlier if moisture is adequate), and sow lentil and vetch early, with lentil sown on 31 March at Wudinna yielding 81% more than 7 May[227](../references.md#r227). Far north and Flinders vegetable gardens probably follow Alice Springs-style heat patterns but with cooler winters [inference]. Do not transplant those dates.

## Soil, cover crops and rotation

### SA Drought Hub orchard-floor trial

- **Sites and treatments:** Riverland and Barossa trials compared herbicide control, a volunteer sward, a pollinator mix, medic plus ryegrass, tall fescue in the tree row and native prostrate saltbush. Sowing rates were not stated[199](../references.md#r199).
- **Practice:** sow the cover in mid-autumn "before the rain arrives", as close to the tree line as possible. Avoid slashing as long as possible, then slash and side-throw as tree-line mulch. Cover seed needs winter rain to establish[199](../references.md#r199) [241](../references.md#r241).
- **Results:** the Barossa six-year under-vine trial had significantly higher N and soil carbon than the herbicide control (no numbers given). First-year Riverland differences were small and not statistically significant. Apple harvest was sampled but no yield results were reported.
- **Water:** tall fescue raised soil carbon but competes for water. The project page warns that overly vigorous covers like fescue may harm soil water in hot, dry climates, while medic and ryegrass may fit better[241](../references.md#r241). For hot SA orchards, the evidence favours medic plus ryegrass where water is limiting [inference]. Evidence: moderate (trial, early results).

### Field-scale pulses and breaks (Eyre Peninsula, Eudunda)

These are broadacre results, not garden rates.

- Lentil PBA Bolt and PBA Hallmark XT were adapted, and Bolt has improved boron and salt tolerance. Vetch Volga performed well across low-rainfall sites. Faba PBA Samira out-yielded PBA Bendoc and matched PBA Marne when sown in May. Semi-leafless field peas (PBA Butler, PBA Wharton) yield more in low rainfall[227](../references.md#r227).
- Early field pea sowing at Eudunda cut yield 15-28% and raised blackspot and frost risk, so May is preferred. Seeding rates could be cut by about a quarter for lentil and vetch at most sites (same source).
- Upper Eyre Peninsula (Minnipa) breaks included canola, field pea, oats, medic, sulla and vetch-oat. Best wheat followed chemical fallow then vetch pasture (2.87 t/ha), versus 1.63-1.69 t/ha for continuous wheat[258](../references.md#r258). Evidence: moderate (trial data).
- Matching legume to soil pH (acid sands to lupin or serradella, alkaline soils to medic, vetch, field pea or faba) is [general knowledge], to be confirmed with PIRSA or SARDI. Lupin suitability on SA acid sands (Fleurieu, Kangaroo Island) is not sourced.
- Nitrogen: legumes fix roughly 20 kg N per tonne of shoot dry matter (derived from approximate table values). Harvesting removes 30-40% of crop N in the seed, so a green manure returns more than a picked bean crop[165](../references.md#r165).
- Home-scale idea: sow faba or field pea as an autumn green manure in plains and Hills gardens [inference]. The garden rates in the sources reviewed come from NSW vineyard work, not SA.

### Riverland irrigation and salinity lessons

- Flood or furrow irrigation over sandy rootzones above clay raised watertables, and evaporation from saline groundwater left salt in topsoil. By the 1970s research showed over-application was the root cause, and better farm-level irrigation management was the main answer[157](../references.md#r157).
- Overhead sprinklers caused leaf salt uptake and defoliation when river salinity peaked, so growers moved to low-level or undertree systems. Recommended practice was to apply only the soil water deficit, monitor with tensiometers or capacitance probes, check sprinkler uniformity and pressure, and filter water. Drainage is a supplement to management (same page, historical, general principles rather than gardener advice).
- Transferable points for a Riverland garden: drip or low-level delivery instead of overhead sprinklers, deep infrequent watering to avoid salt near the surface, and occasional flushing [inference].
- Mulch 5-10 cm may save up to 20% of garden water, but that figure is from Perth's Water Corporation, so treat it as transferable, not SA-verified[171](../references.md#r171).
- Quandong at Quorn is watered 24 L per tree weekly over winter and spring, kept away from the trunk. Arid plants given too much water get sparse heads[74](../references.md#r74).

## Fruit and nut trees here

The national table is on [Fruit and nut trees](../fruit-and-nut-trees.md). No SA-authored care pages were found for citrus, almond, olive, fig, pomegranate, stone fruit, grape, pistachio, persimmon or quince.

- **Citrus (national sources):** spring or autumn in most areas, per SBS Food. A nursery source says spring when soil is about 16 C. Overwatering and poor drainage kill more citrus than drought[339](../references.md#r339) [299](../references.md#r299).
- **Apples and stone fruit (national sources):** plant in winter when dormant; chill need varies by variety. Late frosts can destroy blossom, so avoid frost-prone gullies[340](../references.md#r340). The 10th-percentile winter chill for Lenswood, SA is 91 chill portions (1 March to 31 August, 1911-2012). That compares with Tatura Vic 81 and Spreyton Tas 94[240](../references.md#r240).
- **Olive:** cultivated olives planted and maintained for use are not declared, but feral olive is. See Pests[68](../references.md#r68).
- **Pomegranate and pistachio (WA sources, not SA):** pomegranate bears from about year 3-4 and Mediterranean fruit fly cracks fruit; pistachio takes 6-7 years and is wind-pollinated[61](../references.md#r61) [280](../references.md#r280). These are weak proxies for SA.
- **Quandong** is covered under Native foods.

## Native foods here

The national page is [Native foods](../native-foods.md). Buy from reputable suppliers, and do not wild-harvest.

> **Safety:** Check each plant in a species-specific guide before eating. Kangaroo apple leaves and unripe fruit contain solasodine. Treat unripe bush tomato as unsafe unless a specific source says otherwise. Blanch warrigal greens, which contain oxalates. Follow the product label or permit for any spray.

### Quandong

- **Site:** sand, sandy loam and gravelly ridges. One grower reported good results on alkaline sandy loam. Wetter conditions cause skin splitting and root disease[156](../references.md#r156).
- **Hosts:** quandong is a root hemiparasite. Hosts used include *Myoporum parvifolium* (good in nursery trials), kikuyu, clovers, prostrate lucerne, acacias and melaleucas[156](../references.md#r156). In a CSIRO glasshouse study, *Myoporum parvifolium* and old man saltbush gave significantly greater quandong dry mass than quandong alone, and quandong did not harm the hosts[42](../references.md#r42). An Alice Springs grower lists hop bush, narrow-leaf senna, ruby saltbush, red bud mallee, ghost gum and myoporum[248](../references.md#r248). Evidence: moderate (one trial plus grower accounts). Whether common hosts fix nitrogen was not found.
- **Orchard:** spacing 4-6 m between rows and 2-4 m within rows; pollinator trees about 1:8 in clonal orchards; about 80% seedling and 20% grafted. Flowering is late autumn, fruit ripens from late winter and harvest is usually spring (same fact sheet).
- **Pest:** the fact sheet says the main pest is quandong moth, controlled with dimethoate under a permit held by the Australian Quandong Industry Association. Registration status was not verified here, so follow the product label or permit. Destroying fallen fruit reduces larvae. Herbicides can pass from weeds to quandongs through shared roots.
- **Quorn orchard:** 300 trees interplanted with olives, planted in the 1960s. Grafting success ranged from 70% on a small group to 6 of 100 on a larger one, and seed germination was best around 18 C. Do not spray crowns, because insects pollinate. Pick ripe fruit on the ground or loose. Dried fruit keeps up to 10 years[74](../references.md#r74). Evidence: weak-moderate (one grower).
- A quandong and old man saltbush pairing is an inference from two separate sources, with no field trial found [inference].

### Other natives

- **Muntries** (*Kunzea pomifera*) is native to SA and Victoria, including Yorke Peninsula to the Victorian border, on sandy, often limestone soils with about 500-800 mm of rain. It needs well-drained soil and does not tolerate waterlogging. The source is Wikipedia (secondary), and its fruit-colour description is internally inconsistent[345](../references.md#r345).
- **Old man saltbush** suits 175-500 mm rain and is moderately salt tolerant. Leaves can hold up to 28% salt, and summer waterlogging can kill it. Use as human food was not covered[60](../references.md#r60). Native prostrate saltbush was trialled as an orchard floor cover in the Riverland, with no performance result found.
- **Wattleseed:** *Acacia victoriae* is the most common edible wattle[262](../references.md#r262). A Quorn grower estimated the national industry at about $40m by 2025 (the grower's own estimate). Roasting, toxic *Acacia* species and weed risk were not covered[72](../references.md#r72).
- **Bush tomato:** a small thorny shrub of arid Australia, grown commercially by Aboriginal communities including at Nepabunna and Murray Bridge, per Wikipedia (secondary) [347](../references.md#r347).
- **Kangaroo apple** is naturalised in SA. Weed potential is not stated by the source, so check state weed lists before planting[346](../references.md#r346).
- **SGA list of southern natives:** coastal pigface (dry, well-drained, full sun), warrigal greens (moist free-draining soil, part shade, control spread), nodding saltbush, native raspberry, with only growing conditions given. No edible-use or safety detail was read[265](../references.md#r265).
- **Quorn and Indigenous context:** a Quorn bush-food event listed quandong (kurti, a Nukunu name), muntries, mountain pepper, lemon myrtle and bush tomato. The Kurti Festival is run by Quorn Community Landcare Group with the SA Arid Lands Landscape Board and Future Drought Fund support, and a Nukunu cultural knowledge workshop was held. No Indigenous-owned enterprise was described[72](../references.md#r72). No SA Indigenous-led enterprise was verified.
- **Pollinators:** native vegetation affects crop pollinator numbers up to about 200 m into the crop. That Hort Innovation work was in three South Australian cropping regions, and pest control was not established[232](../references.md#r232).

## Pests and biosecurity here

See [insect pests](../pests/insects.md), [diseases, weeds and soil problems](../pests/diseases.md) and [Biosecurity and weeds](../biosecurity-and-weeds.md).

### Fruit fly rules and zones

> **Check before you move produce:** Fruit fly rules change during outbreaks. The PIRSA pages carry no declaration dates, so check the detections map on PIRSA fruit fly[153](../references.md#r153) before moving or giving away fruit and vegetables.

- **Current status (as fetched):** Queensland fruit fly detections continue in the Riverland with an eradication response. Elizabeth East is described as metropolitan Adelaide's only remaining outbreak zone, with sterile fly release[153](../references.md#r153).
- **Permanent rules:** restrictions apply to bringing or posting produce and plant material into SA and the Riverland Pest Free Area. Outbreak restrictions limit moving certain fruit and vegetables within red and yellow zones regardless of origin. In higher-risk Riverland areas, do not move fruit and vegetables out of the area. Fruit Fly Officers inspect backyards, and a Riverland "Pick and Collect" trial supports residents in high-risk areas[153](../references.md#r153).
- **Fines:** fines rise from 1 July 2026, but the amount is not on the page. An older PIRSA headline mentions a $417 fine, which is likely out of date[155](../references.md#r155).
- **Zones:** the red outbreak area has a 1.5 km radius (eradication; officers may remove fruit and vegetables, treat soil, bait and release sterile flies). The yellow suspension area is 15 km for Queensland fruit fly and 7.5 km for Mediterranean fruit fly. Restrictions apply only to "restricted fruit and vegetables", and that list is not on the page[150](../references.md#r150).
- **Pest Free Area:** it is suspended during outbreaks and reinstated only after trading partners recognise eradication, which can be after the outbreak end date (same page).
- **Advice:** anyone in or near a red or yellow zone should assume home-grown host fruit and tomatoes may not leave the zone [inference]. Spring tidy-up of fallen fruit and thinning is PIRSA's recommended action as fly activity rises [inference].
- **Disposal:** bagging fallen fruit in the sun for 3-7 days (Davidson plum advice) and two weeks (Alice Springs advice) are not SA outbreak-zone disposal rules[269](../references.md#r269) [247](../references.md#r247).
- Vinehealth publishes winegrape movement requirements for Queensland fruit fly, which were not read[245](../references.md#r245).

### Declared weeds popular with gardeners

- **Feral olive** is a declared weed; cultivated olives planted and maintained for use are not. It crowds out grassy woodland understorey, raises bushfire risk, spreads by birds, foxes and contaminated soil or dumped green waste, and resprouts from the lignotuber. Hand-pull seedlings (6 cm to 1.5 m) in winter in moist soil, or use cut-and-swab, drill-and-fill or spot spray. Avoid chemical use in hot periods, and waterway work may need a Water Affecting Activity permit[68](../references.md#r68). PIRSA's wilding olive page lists herbicide methods and permits but did not state declaration or sale rules[158](../references.md#r158). Feral olive wildings are a local issue on Kangaroo Island (headline only seen) [73](../references.md#r73). Stopping fruit drop and not dumping green waste follows from the spread pathways [inference].
- **Boneseed** is a declared weed and a Weed of National Significance. Green Adelaide landowners must take reasonable steps to control it, and sale is prohibited in the Green Adelaide region. It spreads by birds, so clean soil off shoes and tools[26](../references.md#r26).
- **Sale and movement:** declared plants cannot be bought, swapped, sold, brought into SA or transported on public roads (section 188), and fines up to $60,000 for individuals were reported. Listed plants include feral olive, horehound, blackberry and Bundy blackberry, azarola and hawthorn, water caltrop, and asparagus weeds (bridal creeper, ground and ornamental asparagus). Some declared grasses (buffel, African lovegrass, serrated tussock) could appear in seed mixes[76](../references.md#r76). That page is a regional summary, so check the statewide list (not read) [164](../references.md#r164).
- **Almond, tagasaste and fennel** were not on the banned-from-sale page as read. That is absence from a page, not clearance. Check the statewide declared list and your landscape board before planting any of them, or any cover-crop species.
