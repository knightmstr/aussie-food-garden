# Tasmania

> **How reliable is this page?** Biosecurity (fruit fly status, declared weeds), BoM frost counts, the VegNET cover-crop trial and the Aboriginal-owned native food business are well sourced. The planting calendars are not: they come from a garden app, a nursery, a seed company, a 2011 personal website and a botanic garden article, and they disagree on several crops, so both readings are shown. Hobart has the most data. North and north-west Tasmania and the uplands have no region-specific calendar at all, and the two frost-date sources conflict by about six weeks, so check BoM data and your own site records.

### Frost facts, as the sources state them

- BoM says few long-term, high-quality frost records exist in northern Tasmania, and gives frost counts, not first and last dates. BoM North TAS[23](../references.md#r23)
- BoM potential frost day maps (below 2, 0, -2 and -5 C; 1976-2005 averages) show frost is most common in alpine NSW, Victoria and Tasmania, mostly late autumn to early spring. The maps are images, so no town values were read. BoM frost maps[24](../references.md#r24)
- **Conflict:** Fryd (Hobart) says last frost about mid-November and first frost early April. Airtasker (Tasmania as a whole) says last frost late December and first frost late February. The two differ by about six weeks. Neither is government, and the Airtasker row is statewide, so it is unusable for Hobart. Fryd's "Zone 10a" label is a US-style zone that looks unreliable for Hobart, and its dates look modelled. Treat both as unverified. Fryd[308](../references.md#r308); Airtasker[281](../references.md#r281)
- Local rule of thumb, not a frost date: City of Hobart says plant tomatoes out after Show Day (late October) to avoid October winds and last frosts. The Royal Tasmanian Botanical Gardens article quotes the same rule (22 October, Hobart Show Day) but the author questions it, and says Tasmanian conditions vary widely over short distances, so keep site records. City of Hobart[175](../references.md#r175) (council); RTBG[163](../references.md#r163)

## Soil, cover crops and rotation

### VegNET Tasmania cover-crop demonstration

AUSVEG reported in February 2021 that six cover-crop mixes were planted in early 2020 at Hagley Farm School in northern Tasmania. The purposes were nematode suppression, feed, weed suppression, soil conditioning, pathogen control and nitrogen fixation. AUSVEG VegNET Tasmania[218](../references.md#r218) (industry)

- The nitrogen mix included lupins, vetch, tick beans and peas.
- The nematode mix was Nemat and Terranova radish, said to suppress nematodes by releasing isothiocyanates.
- Compaction was simulated with machinery traffic. Non-trafficked areas yielded better in every mix except the soil-conditioning mix, which gave more wet weight under compaction, attributed to its large taproots.
- A follow-on project (to June 2022, funded through Smart Farming) looks at cover crops in intensive rotations. The article compared spraying with crimping to terminate crops but did not report results.
- This is a demonstration, so evidence is weak to moderate. Sowing windows by region and soil type were not given. A fuller report (VG16068) was surfaced but not read.

### General covers and timing

- In cool-winter areas (Tasmania is named), sow cool-season green manures in early autumn, or after autumn rain if dry; early spring also works. Cut at the start of flowering and wait 4 to 6 weeks before sowing. Biofumigants must be dug in. Warm-season covers (buckwheat, cowpea, millets and others) go in after frost. SGA green manure[270](../references.md#r270) (practitioner)
- Brassica biofumigants and covers can raise clubroot risk in infected beds. This matters for Tasmanian brassica growers. VG15010[236](../references.md#r236)
- Residues: oat straw is about 70:1 carbon to nitrogen and cereal rye straw about 82:1, so they can tie up nitrogen; radish and mustards are about 14 to 15:1 and break down quickly. AUSVEG/SoilWealth[222](../references.md#r222)

### Rotation and soil problems

- SGA's 4-bed cycle: legume, then heavy feeder, then light feeder, then green manure, with beds staggered. Rules: no same-family crops back to back, no heavy feeder after a heavy feeder, green manure at least once every four seasons. SGA crop rotation[267](../references.md#r267)
- Sample rotation [inference, adapted from SGA]: autumn broad bean or pea; then tomato or pumpkin the next spring; then carrot, beetroot or onion; then oats or buckwheat green manure, cut before seed set. Brassicas on their own long cycle.
- Clubroot: spores persist up to 20 years; lime to pH 7.0 to 7.5 at least a week before transplanting; control shepherd's purse and wild radish (evidence: strong). VG15010[236](../references.md#r236)
- Late blight outbreaks are concentrated in northern Tasmania among other areas; infected tubers and volunteer potatoes are the main source, and kangaroo apple and nightshades are indicator weeds. AUSVEG PT04010[216](../references.md#r216)
- Not sourced: Tasmanian soil types, acidity and liming, and sowing rates.

## Fruit and nut trees here

For the national table see [Fruit and nut trees](../fruit-and-nut-trees.md).

- Tasmania's apples come 83% from the Huon district, with Spreyton and the Tamar the rest. Australian Apples (APAL) [209](../references.md#r209)
- Winter chill: Tasmania achieves higher chill than other states, up to 40% more than Western Australia. 10th-percentile winter chill (chill portions, March to August, 1911-2012): Spreyton 94 and Huonville 105. UTAS dormancy fact sheet[203](../references.md#r203); Horticulture Innovation fact sheet[240](../references.md#r240)
- Hobart nursery advice (Stoneman's, commercial): bare-root fruit trees and raspberry canes are available in June; prune apple, pear, quince and nuts in winter; prune cherry straight after summer fruiting; winter is good for berries and fruiting bushes. Copper spray at bud swell and again at about 80% petal fall, repeated about every 2 weeks in wet Tasmanian springs. Follow the product label. Stoneman's pruning[320](../references.md#r320); Stoneman's seasonal guide[321](../references.md#r321)
- RTBG: apples, pears and cherries need a cold period; avoid heavy pruning of trees and large shrubs for a couple of months in spring because of sap flow. RTBG[162](../references.md#r162)
- Nuts (NSW DPI factsheets, not Tasmania-specific): hazelnut is theoretically feasible on the Flinders and King Islands (48 to 60 chill portions; late spring frosts damage female flowers); walnut suits parts of Tasmania (the factsheet's text says 48 to 60 portions, its table 54 to 72); chestnut suits most of Tasmania (58 to 88 chill portions; wind pollinated, use several cultivars). NSW DPI hazelnut[88](../references.md#r88); walnut[98](../references.md#r98); chestnut[82](../references.md#r82)
- Quince (NSW DPI): avoid valley floors and frost pockets; plant late June to early July when dormant. NSW DPI quince[93](../references.md#r93)
- Not sourced: cherry, berry, kiwiberry and hop care in Tasmania. A Tasmanian government cherry fact sheet was surfaced but not read.

## Native foods here

For national coverage see [Native foods](../native-foods.md).

> **Safety:** do not wild-harvest. Buy plants or products from a reliable source, and check the safety notes below. Harvesting in nature reserves and national parks is illegal (Milkwood, blog).

- **Palawa Kipli.** A Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre release (July 2026) describes it as "a 100% Aboriginal owned business", run by Business Manager Kitana Mansell (palawa). Its Native 9-Spice blend, launched during NAIDOC Week at Piyura Kitina/Risdon Cove, uses nine Tasmanian ingredients: kunzea (tinputina), pepperberry (tapu), wattleseed (wurayni), saltbush (taytina), kelp (rikawa), sea salt, river mint, sea parsley and round-leaf mint bush, reflecting the nine nations. Profits go to the Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre. It is made with a non-Aboriginal partner (Zea Gourmet). This page claims nothing further about the organisation. Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre[387](../references.md#r387)
- **Mountain pepper (*Tasmannia lanceolata*; pepperberry).** Yarra Ranges Council (Victoria): moist soils on slopes and gullies in wet tall forest or rainforest, semi-shade to dappled shade, "frost and snow tolerant", a dense screening shrub that responds to pruning, shy to flower at lower altitudes; leaves and fruit supply peppery flavour to the bush foods industry. ANBG: cultivation "generally easy", prefers well-composted soil and half to full shade, propagated from cuttings. Neither page gives a toxicity or preparation warning, which is not the same as saying it is safe. ANBG's red-berry statement should not be relied on (berries are black when ripe in many sources) [general knowledge]. Male and female plants are separate in this genus [general knowledge, not confirmed on the pages read]. Yarra Ranges Council[192](../references.md#r192); ANBG[14](../references.md#r14)
- **Wattle.** Edible wattles occur in every ecosystem including Tasmania; at least 150 of 1,146 Acacia species were used by Indigenous Australians as food, medicine or materials. Roasting, toxic species and weed risk were not covered. AgriFutures[207](../references.md#r207)
- **Kangaroo apple (*Solanum aviculare*)** is naturalised in Tasmania. Its leaves and unripe fruit contain the toxic alkaloid solasodine, and the source did not say whether ripe fruit is safe. Check weed status before planting. Wikipedia[346](../references.md#r346) (secondary)
- Murnong is a staple root food across south-east Australia, but nothing in the sources reviewed confirms Tasmanian species or cultivation. See the [Victoria page](vic.md) for the murnong notes and the verified Victorian nursery.

## Pests and biosecurity here

See also [insect pests](../pests/insects.md), [diseases, weeds and soil problems](../pests/diseases.md) and [biosecurity and weeds](../biosecurity-and-weeds.md).

### Fruit fly: Tasmania is a pest-free area

- Queensland fruit fly was detected in northern Tasmania in 2018 and "quickly eradicated". Tasmania is an internationally recognised fruit fly pest-free area, kept that way by strict import requirements, targeted inspections and passenger, luggage, freight and mail checks. NRE Tasmania fruit fly[80](../references.md#r80) (government)
- The page does not list prohibited items. It points to the Traveller's Guide and the Plant Biosecurity Manual Tasmania for host produce. Rules on what fruit and vegetables you may bring in are likely to be strict [inference], but fines, host lists and any current control-zone status were not read. Check the NRE Tasmania pages before travelling with or sending produce.
- **If you suspect fruit fly:** report it to Biosecurity Tasmania on (03) 6165 3777 (free; report even if unsure). Keep maggoty fruit sealed and refrigerated for collection. Vinegar flies are commonly confused with fruit fly. Home-garden control steps for Victoria are on the [Victoria page](vic.md), but in Tasmania the first step is to report, not to treat [inference from the reporting advice].
- One search result noted 2018-era control zones and a later "fruit fly free" declaration. This was not verified.

### Declared weeds

- Example: *Erica* species are declared under the Tasmanian Biosecurity Act 2019. Importation, sale and distribution are prohibited. Zones (eradication or containment) depend on the municipality and are set out in the statutory weed management plan. Contact Biosecurity Tasmania on (03) 6165 3777. NRE Tasmania Erica[79](../references.md#r79) (government)
- **Older pages may be superseded.** Search also returned older material referring to the Weed Management Act 1999 and a 2017 Declared Weeds Order. Confirm the current legislation and your municipality's zone with Biosecurity Tasmania before relying on any older page.
- Kangaroo apple is naturalised in Tasmania; check declared weed status before planting. [inference from the naturalisation note]

### Pests and diseases

> **Read first:** follow the product label or permit for any spray. Spray late in the day and avoid flowering plants to protect bees.

- **Brassica caterpillars and aphids** rise in summer (cabbage white and moth caterpillars, cabbage aphid). The RTBG article suggests *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Dipel). Check the label for registered use. RTBG[163](../references.md#r163)
- **Late blight** outbreaks are concentrated in northern Tasmania among other places. AUSVEG PT04010[216](../references.md#r216)
- **Fruit tree sprays:** see Stoneman's copper timing above. Copper efficacy for blight is ungraded in the sources reviewed.

## Gaps and what to check locally

- **Calendars:** no council, NRE Tasmania, Tasmanian Community Gardens Network or Gardenate calendar was read for any Tasmanian region. Taroona Neighbourhood Garden's calendar page is links only. - **Fruit fly and imports:** traveller rules, the Plant Biosecurity Manual host list and any control-zone status were not retrieved. Check NRE Tasmania.
- **Weeds:** only *Erica* was read. The NRE declared weeds index, NRM South booklets and council pages were not opened.
- **Soil:** no Tasmanian soil, acid soil or liming guidance, and no sowing rates for covers. The VegNET follow-on results and the VG16068 report were not read.
- **Fruit and nuts:** cherries, berries, kiwiberry and hops are unsourced for Tasmania. No pollination or pruning windows for nuts.
- **Native foods:** Tasmanian-specific edibles (native currants, apple berry, native cherry, sea celery, kelp), other Aboriginal-led enterprises and safety notes for pepperberry and wattleseed processing were not verified.
- **Crop pairings:** Tasmania-specific companion and rotation data were not sourced.
