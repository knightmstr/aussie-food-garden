# Diseases, weeds and soil problems: organic management

> **Read first:** use any product exactly as its label or permit says; registration status is only given where a source shows it, and none was checked on the APVMA database. Spray late in the day and avoid flowering plants to protect bees: a [PIRSA/SARDI technote](https://pir.sa.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/497543/Naturalure-Bee-Safety-Technote.pdf) quotes label wording "do not spray on plants in flower if bees are active" (for an insect bait; the late-afternoon timing is a practical rule from the project's insect notes, not a disease-product label). Follow the product label or permit.

> **How reliable is this page?** Government and levy-funded sources cover clubroot, late blight, peach leaf curl, anthracnose of beans, downy mildew, damping-off, wilts, white rot, myrtle rust, blossom end rot, frost fleece and water repellence. Many home-garden remedies have no Australian trial. Several sources are broadacre, vineyard, overseas or retail pages, and the page says so where it matters. The Australian Certified Organic Standard (ACOS) details come from a document that was only partly read (see [Organic certification](#organic-certification-acos-partly-read)).

## The short version

- Prevention does most of the work: dry leaves, wider spacing, clean seed and transplants, removing old crop residue, and resistant varieties are the best-supported tools for most leaf diseases ([Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/downy-mildew), [Agriculture Victoria](https://agriculture.vic.gov.au/biosecurity/plant-diseases/vegetable-diseases/anthracnose-of-beans), [AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf)).
- Monitor often. Organic fungicides are protectants: copper and sulphur have little effect once infection has begun ([Hort Innovation](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-biopesticides-in-australia-fact-sheet.pdf)).
- Soil-borne diseases (clubroot, wilts, white rot) cannot be cured. Spores or bacteria last 10 to 20 years, so use containers, new beds, grafted plants or resistant varieties ([Hort Innovation clubroot](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-clubroot-management-in-brassica-vegetables-fact-sheet.pdf), [Hort Innovation soilborne guide](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-soilborne-disease-in-vegetable-crops-guide.pdf)).
- Get the cause right before treating: blossom end rot is mainly irregular water, not missing calcium ([NT Agnote 479](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/233618/479.pdf)); sooty mould is a sign of sap-sucking insects ([Yates](https://www.yates.com.au/garden-hub/sooty-mould/), retail source).
- Beneficials: the sources reviewed give no sourced natural enemies for diseases. For insects see [insect pests](../pests/insects.md).
- Physical controls: fleece for frost, clear plastic for solarising potting mix, grafted plants, and removing infected plants all have sources below.
- Softest product last. "Organic-permitted" is about certification, not proof that a product works or is harmless; copper builds up in soil ([AIH](https://aih.org.au/?p=340)) [inference on the wording].
- Treat the evidence grades as the guide. Folklore and home recipes are labelled as such.

## Fungal and oomycete diseases

### Powdery mildew

**Recognise:** powdery white growth on leaves of cucurbits, roses, grapes and many ornamentals ([Hort Innovation VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf)). In the NT it is the cooler-season mildew of cucumber ([NT Agnote B4](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/233384/352.pdf)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=powdery+mildew+leaves&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** use the airflow, spacing and sanitation steps listed under [Downy mildew](#downy-mildew); the sources there are general foliar-disease advice, not specific to powdery mildew.
**Beneficials and biological control:** AUSVEG states there are no registered or consistently effective biological control agents for the mildews ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf)).
**Physical controls:** none sourced beyond removing affected leaves.
**Organic sprays and products:** greenhouse cucumber trials found potassium bicarbonate reduced severity when applied before or after inoculation, but less than a synthetic fungicide at 14 days, and adding oil did not improve it ([Hort Innovation VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf)). Oils were the best eradicants but the effect faded after about a day; a petroleum oil was the best protectant, lasting up to 7 days (same report). Milk was not significant (see [Milk spray](#milk-spray)). A 2% copper product alternated with a synthetic fungicide was among the better programs; copper alone was not clearly shown (same report, text truncated). Details: [Potassium bicarbonate](#potassium-bicarbonate), [Sulphur](#sulphur), [Copper and lime sulphur](#copper-and-lime-sulphur).
**Evidence:** moderate, but one greenhouse cucumber trial. [inference] Frame sprays as "reduce, not cure", combined with airflow and removing infected leaves; the repeat interval from trials is not sourced (label intervals are under the products).
**Sources:** [Hort Innovation VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf), [AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf).

### Downy mildew

**Recognise:** white downy growth on leaf undersides in moist weather, then brown spots and dead areas; hard to control once advanced. Different species attack cucurbits, grape, lettuce and onion ([Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/downy-mildew)). In the NT it is the wet-season mildew of cucumber ([NT Agnote B4](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/233384/352.pdf)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=downy+mildew+leaves&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** favoured by warm, moist weather; spores blow in from older crops and survive in soil and residue. Use resistant varieties, remove or plough in old crops promptly, space plants wider ([Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/downy-mildew)). AUSVEG adds clean transplants, weed control, checking older leaves, avoiding morning watering and high humidity, leaf trimming and ventilation, and (lettuce) avoiding high ammonium nitrate ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none registered or consistently effective ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf)).
**Physical controls:** remove old crop residue; do not leave it near new plantings ([Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/downy-mildew)).
**Organic sprays and products:** copper "may be effective on some infections"; sulphur is not suitable for all crops, especially in hot weather ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf)). The Hort Innovation greenhouse report did not test any product on downy mildew ([VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf)). Do not rely on spraying once disease is advanced.
**Evidence:** moderate to strong for cultural measures (government advice); weak to moderate for copper (no efficacy figures).
**Sources:** [Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/downy-mildew), [AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf).

### Late blight

**Recognise:** potato and tomato disease caused by Phytophthora infestans; favoured by still, warm, humid weather (old strains need about 8 hours of leaf wetness at 15 C, optimum about 22 C) ([AUSVEG PT04010](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/PT04010.pdf)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=late+blight+tomato+Phytophthora+infestans&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** main inoculum is infected tubers, volunteer potatoes and cull piles, and infected tubers can carry symptomless mycelium. Remove and destroy affected plants; do not irrigate blighted crops; do not mow infected crops (it releases spores); deep-bury haulm at an approved site. Cultivars with useful foliar and tuber resistance (overseas ratings): Admiral, Atlantic, Charlotte, Innovator, Midas, Nooksack, Norland, Orla, Osprey, Sebago, Simcoe, Sini, Symfonia, Trent, Valentina, Victoria ([AUSVEG PT04010](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/PT04010.pdf)). Kangaroo apple and nightshades act as indicator weeds (same source).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** hygiene as above.
**Organic sprays and products:** the AUSVEG document does not mention copper, so copper efficacy for blight is ungraded.
**Evidence:** moderate (industry technical document). Recent outbreaks were concentrated in northern Tasmania, Victoria and Mt Gambier SA (same source). [inference] It is a low priority for tropical gardeners.
**Sources:** [AUSVEG PT04010](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/PT04010.pdf).

### Anthracnose of beans

**Recognise:** angular reddish-brown spots along leaf veins, dark sunken lesions on stems and pods, seed lesions, possible pink-orange spore ooze ([Agriculture Victoria](https://agriculture.vic.gov.au/biosecurity/plant-diseases/vegetable-diseases/anthracnose-of-beans)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=bean+anthracnose&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** infected seed is the main route; rain, overhead irrigation and wind spread it; symptoms are best at 18-25 C. Use certified or disease-free-history seed, leave at least 2 years before beans return after an infected crop, remove diseased plants, and do not cultivate or harvest wet infected crops. Tweed Wonder, Wellington Wonder and Redlands Beauty resist the most common race but can be badly affected by less common races (same source).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** removing diseased plants (same source).
**Organic sprays and products:** none sourced. Anthracnose of mango, avocado and other crops was not sourced (see [Gaps](#gaps)).
**Evidence:** strong (government).
**Sources:** [Agriculture Victoria](https://agriculture.vic.gov.au/biosecurity/plant-diseases/vegetable-diseases/anthracnose-of-beans).

### Rust

**Recognise:** a garlic rust story describes slight leaf discolouration spreading on wind-borne spores, linked by the gardener to excess moisture, crowding and heavy clay ([Young Witness](https://www.youngwitness.com.au/story/6882268/winning-the-battle-against-garlic-rust/), a regional news story, weak-anecdotal).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=rust+fungus+leaves&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** the same gardener tried pruning leaves (not enough alone), morning watering, drip irrigation, compost or mounds in clay (not enough alone), containers, sterilising tools, and about a seven-year rotation for garlic in an infected bed (same source). General airflow and wetness advice from the mildew sources is not rust-specific.
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** as above.
**Organic sprays and products:** the sulphur label lists bean rust among vegetable uses at up to 2 kg/ha; verify on the physical label ([OCP Ecosulfur label](https://ocp.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ecosulfur-label-frontback.pdf)). The potassium bicarbonate label lists rust of geraniums at 40 g per 10 L plus 30 mL emulsified vegetable oil every 7 days; label claim only, no trial found ([OCP Eco-Fungicide label](https://ozbreed.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/eco-fungicide-500g-label4.4.18.pdf)).
**Evidence:** weak-anecdotal for garlic; label claims only for products. Hollyhock rust was not sourced.
**Sources:** [Young Witness](https://www.youngwitness.com.au/story/6882268/winning-the-battle-against-garlic-rust/), [OCP Ecosulfur label](https://ocp.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ecosulfur-label-frontback.pdf), [OCP Eco-Fungicide label](https://ozbreed.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/eco-fungicide-500g-label4.4.18.pdf).

### Black spot of roses

**Recognise:** black spots, often with a yellow ring, that enlarge; leaves yellow and fall and the bush can defoliate; worst in warm, humid or wet weather ([Burnside area garden group page](https://www.baag.com.au/?p=25451)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=black+spot+rose&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** water the soil not the leaves, keep the root zone consistently moist, clear fallen leaves in autumn and winter (the fungus overwinters on the ground), feed every 6-8 weeks with extra potash, choose resistant varieties, prune off infected material early. Do not compost infected material or keep a persistently infected rose (same source).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** leaf clean-up and pruning as above.
**Organic sprays and products:** the potassium bicarbonate label lists rose black spot at 40 g per 10 L with 20 mL emulsified vegetable oil every 10-14 days; label claim only ([OCP Eco-Fungicide label](https://ozbreed.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/eco-fungicide-500g-label4.4.18.pdf)). The sulphur label's "black spot (scab)" for pome fruit is apple scab, a different disease ([OCP Ecosulfur label](https://ocp.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ecosulfur-label-frontback.pdf)).
**Evidence:** weak to moderate (local garden group, not government); product use is label-claim only.
**Sources:** [Burnside area garden group](https://www.baag.com.au/?p=25451), [OCP Eco-Fungicide label](https://ozbreed.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/eco-fungicide-500g-label4.4.18.pdf).

### White rot

**Recognise:** onion, garlic and leek roots rot brown to black at the stem base, with fluffy white growth and poppy-seed-sized black sclerotia; pink root can look similar ([AUSVEG white rot](https://ausveg.com.au/knowledge-hub/developing-strategies-to-inhibit-onion-white-rot/)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=onion+white+rot+Sclerotium+cepivorum&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** sclerotia can stay dormant up to 20 years and germinate only near Allium roots; one per 10 kg of soil can start an outbreak; spread is mainly by soil, equipment and flowing water; most active in cool moist soil (about 7-22 C) and strongly inhibited above 25 C. Plant later when soil is warmer (trade-off: later harvest), use free-draining soil and strong-rooted varieties, and limit soil movement (same source). [inference] Do not plant bought-in sets or move soil from unknown sources, and do not replant Alliums in infested soil.
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** hygiene and limiting soil movement.
**Organic sprays and products:** germination stimulants such as onion juice or garlic extract may reduce soil pressure, but rates and timing are still being trialled (same source). WA DPIRD has a white-rot-in-garlic biosecurity alert page that was not read: [DPIRD WA](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/plant-biosecurity/biosecurity-alert-white-rot-garlic?nopaging=1).
**Evidence:** moderate for the biology and cultural measures; weak and experimental for stimulants.
**Sources:** [AUSVEG white rot](https://ausveg.com.au/knowledge-hub/developing-strategies-to-inhibit-onion-white-rot/).

### Peach leaf curl

**Recognise:** young leaves turn pink-red, thicken and curl and may drop; fruit can show raised rough areas. Favoured by a cool wet spring at budswell, then warm humid growth; fungal optimum 20-26 C. Affects peach and nectarine, also apricot and almond ([NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/1580067/Peach-leaf-curl.pdf)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=peach+leaf+curl&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** maintain tree vigour, thin fruit more heavily, irrigate adequately and give extra nitrogen (NSW DPI). Bin fallen leaves, remove distorted leaves and fruit, choose less susceptible varieties (none named); untreated trees worsen each year ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/peach-leaf-curl/)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** no biological control agents are known ([NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/1580067/Peach-leaf-curl.pdf)).
**Physical controls:** leaf clean-up as above.
**Organic sprays and products:** protectant sprays in early spring, particularly copper, are "vital", and some copper fungicides are approved for organic systems (NSW DPI). Sustainable Gardening Australia advises copper oxychloride or lime sulphur in winter before bud burst, well before symptoms, preferring lime sulphur because copper builds up in soil ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/peach-leaf-curl/)). Spraying after symptoms appear is not said to cure existing leaves [inference from the "well before" wording]. See [Copper and lime sulphur](#copper-and-lime-sulphur).
**Evidence:** strong (government extension), moderate for the home-gardener detail (SGA).
**Sources:** [NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/1580067/Peach-leaf-curl.pdf), [SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/peach-leaf-curl/).

### Citrus collar rot

**Recognise:** yellow leaves and gum oozing from bark at the trunk base; bark wet and soft, may split; rot can ringbark the tree; autumn to winter; common in clay soils ([Fruit Salad Trees](https://fruitsaladtrees.com/pages/collar-rot), a nursery page).
**Prevent:** keep the bud union well above soil level; if drainage is uncertain plant on top of the ground and mound; keep mulch clear of the trunk; do not wet the trunk, overwater or injure the lower trunk (same source). DPIRD WA says a Phytophthora-susceptible rootstock should not go in heavy clay or poorly drained soil ([DPIRD WA citrus guide 2025](https://dpird-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1482785/Citrus-plant-protection-guide-2025-web.pdf)). [inference] These cultural fixes come before any copper.
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** cut lesions back to healthy tissue with a sterilised knife, disinfecting between cuts (Fruit Salad Trees).
**Organic sprays and products:** the nursery page suggests copper fungicide on exposed wood, registered copper under-tree sprays in autumn, and a phosphorous acid foliar spray; efficacy not independently verified. Copper oxychloride labels list collar rot (DPIRD WA citrus guide, rates not read).
**Evidence:** weak to moderate (nursery source).
**Sources:** [Fruit Salad Trees](https://fruitsaladtrees.com/pages/collar-rot), [DPIRD WA citrus guide 2025](https://dpird-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1482785/Citrus-plant-protection-guide-2025-web.pdf).

### Citrus scab melanose and black spot

> **Safety:** citrus black spot is treated as a declared pest by NSW DPI and WA DPIRD ([NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/1615788/Citrus-black-spot.pdf), [DPIRD WA](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/plant-biosecurity/citrus-black-spot-declared-pest); both pages were found but not read). [inference] If you suspect it, contact your state agriculture department rather than treating it yourself.

**Recognise:** not sourced. The recognition pages of the DPIRD WA citrus guide were not read.
**Prevent:** not sourced.
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** none sourced.
**Organic sprays and products:** the DPIRD WA citrus guide says labels of cuprous oxide, copper oxychloride, cupric hydroxide and tribasic copper sulphate list black spot, melanose, smoky blotch, scab and lemon scab; no rates or timing were in the section read ([DPIRD WA citrus guide 2025](https://dpird-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1482785/Citrus-plant-protection-guide-2025-web.pdf)). The sulphur label lists citrus rust mites, bud mite, moss, lichens and melanose at 400-500 g/100 L in July-August in several states; the extracted label text was scrambled, so verify on the physical label ([OCP Ecosulfur label](https://ocp.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ecosulfur-label-frontback.pdf)).
**Evidence:** weak (label and guide listings only).
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Sooty mould

**Recognise:** a secondary black mould growing on honeydew from aphids, scale, mealybugs, whiteflies, psyllids and leafhoppers; it does not feed on the plant but blocks light ([Yates](https://www.yates.com.au/garden-hub/sooty-mould/), retail brand page).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=sooty+mould&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** control the insects first, then wash leaves once honeydew is gone; keep plants watered, sunny and fed (same source). [inference] Washing before the insects are controlled lets the mould return. The ant-honeydew link is not sourced.
**Beneficials and biological control:** see [insect pests](../pests/insects.md) for the sap-suckers.
**Physical controls:** washing leaves, as above.
**Organic sprays and products:** the page recommends its own products, which are not repeated here.
**Evidence:** weak to moderate (retail source).
**Sources:** [Yates](https://www.yates.com.au/garden-hub/sooty-mould/).

### Myrtle rust

**Recognise:** small purple spots on soft young leaves, shoot tips or stems, then bright yellow powdery pustules fading to dull yellow then grey; leaves distort. Hosts include eucalypts, bottlebrush, paperbark, tea tree and lilly pilly, with over 300 species recorded. Lemon myrtle is not named on the page read, so its susceptibility is unverified here ([NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/biosecurity/plant/insect-pests-and-plant-diseases/myrtle-rust)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=myrtle+rust&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** check plants regularly; consider removing healthy highly susceptible plants before infection; wash clothes and gloves; do not replant similar Myrtaceae ([NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/biosecurity/plant/insect-pests-and-plant-diseases/myrtle-rust), [Agriculture Victoria](https://agriculture.vic.gov.au/biosecurity/plant-diseases/shrub-and-tree-diseases/myrtle-rust/treating-myrtle-rust-in-home-gardens)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** do not touch, move or collect material from suspect plants; wet plants before removal if no fungicide is used; bag plants before pulling; sun-dry bagged material 3-4 weeks; hairspray can stick spores on a small plant; do not use infected plants as mulch or in green waste; scrub pots and tools with detergent and dry them (Agriculture Victoria). NSW DPI says spray the plant and neighbours 3-4 days before removal and bag it first.
**Organic sprays and products:** both pages direct gardeners to fungicides registered for myrtle rust (APVMA search) and name no organic product. Whether sulphur, copper or bicarbonate is registered for it is unverified. In severe infections do not rely on fungicide alone, since reinfection is highly likely (Agriculture Victoria).
**Evidence:** moderate (government). [inference] Treat it as a plant-selection and hygiene problem, not a spray problem.
**Sources:** [NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/biosecurity/plant/insect-pests-and-plant-diseases/myrtle-rust), [Agriculture Victoria](https://agriculture.vic.gov.au/biosecurity/plant-diseases/shrub-and-tree-diseases/myrtle-rust/treating-myrtle-rust-in-home-gardens).

## Soil-borne problems

### Clubroot

**Recognise:** wilting, stunting and pale plants above ground; stubby swollen galls on roots. Hits all brassicas including cabbage, broccoli, turnip, radish and canola ([Hort Innovation clubroot fact sheet](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-clubroot-management-in-brassica-vegetables-fact-sheet.pdf)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=clubroot+brassica&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** resting spores survive up to 20 years and there is no effective control once symptoms appear. Keep soil at pH 7.0-7.5 with reactive lime applied at least one week before transplanting; soils below pH 6 raise risk. Calcium nitrate and boron inhibit infection, while ammonium fertilisers acidify and raise risk. Leave more than 7 years between brassica crops (2 years only within an integrated program); control shepherd's purse and wild radish. Risk factors are soil 17-25 C, compaction, wet poorly drained soil and brassicas in the last 2 years. Rocket and some radishes are resistant; turnip, radish and canola are highly susceptible. Resistant cultivars help but resistance can erode (same source). The 7.0-7.5 target is for brassicas only, not other crops.
**Beneficials and biological control:** Bacillus subtilis or Gliocladium catenulatum at transplanting helps only at low disease pressure (same source).
**Physical controls:** quarantine infected areas and reduce runoff; for small infestations remove plants, roots and attached soil and burn or send to industrial landfill; avoid overwatering, improve drainage, use raised beds (same source). [inference] Containers with fresh mix or raised beds are the practical fallback in a home garden.
**Organic sprays and products:** fungicides do not cure established infection. Do not use brassica cover or biofumigation crops in clubroot-risk soil: they increase risk (same source).
**Evidence:** strong (Hort Innovation, Werribee Vic trials) for lime and rotation; moderate for calcium nitrate and boron; weak to moderate for Bacillus.
**Sources:** [Hort Innovation clubroot fact sheet](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-clubroot-management-in-brassica-vegetables-fact-sheet.pdf).

### Fusarium wilt

**Recognise:** leaves yellow and drop and plants wilt over days; cut stems show reddish-brown discolouration. In tomato, yellowing runs from the lower leaves up, often on one side, and the top wilts by day and recovers at night ([Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/fusarium-wilt-yellows), [PIRSA](https://pir.sa.gov.au/crops-and-plants/weeds-and-plant-pests/plant-pests/pests-and-diseases-of-significant-concern/fusarium_wilt_of_tomato)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fusarium+wilt&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** the fungus travels in contaminated soil, seed and airborne spores and can persist for decades without a host. Keep it out: avoid contaminated seed, equipment, footwear and water; use resistant varieties or rootstocks where available. Root-knot nematodes raise infection risk. It is favoured by about 28 C, dry weather, acidic soil and ammonium nitrate on fast-growing plants (PIRSA). For brassica yellows avoid ammonium fertilisers on seedlings, and calcium may help suppress disease ([Hort Innovation soilborne guide](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-soilborne-disease-in-vegetable-crops-guide.pdf)). The NT manual lists a Fusarium-tolerant basil (Nufar F1) and a Fusarium-resistant snake bean rootstock (Iron cowpea) ([NT Vegetable growing manual](https://industry.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/228007/ntg-vegetable-growing-manual.pdf)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** use sterilised soil and clean benches, tools, crates and footwear (PIRSA). Do not move soil or plants from infested beds.
**Organic sprays and products:** none sourced.
**Evidence:** moderate (government); resistance is the strongest tool in all three sources, and no resistant home-garden tomato list was found. PIRSA presents Fusarium wilt of tomato as a reportable pest in South Australia (Exotic Plant Pest Hotline 1800 084 881).
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Bacterial wilt

**Recognise:** white ooze when cut tubers or stems are pressed (potato, tomato, eggplant, capsicum, chilli); hot wet weather speeds wilting ([Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/bacterial-wilt)). Wilting and yellowing within days at 25-35 C in moist soil; a stem in water shows a milky stream; bacteria survive over 10 years in soil ([Hort Innovation soilborne guide](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-soilborne-disease-in-vegetable-crops-guide.pdf)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=bacterial+wilt&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** tolerant or resistant varieties; certified whole seed (potato); keep hosts off a site for at least two years and preferably five; do not crop infected soil through summer; keep soil pH 5.5-7, improve drainage, avoid over-irrigation; it spreads in irrigation and rain water downhill and survives in residues and weed hosts (Business Queensland, Hort Innovation).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** use transplants grafted onto resistant rootstock where available (both sources). The NT manual advises raising tomatoes in a protected nursery, not direct-seeding, and using bacterial-wilt-resistant wild Malay eggplant rootstock ([NT Vegetable growing manual](https://industry.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/228007/ntg-vegetable-growing-manual.pdf)); NT Government also flags grafting for tomato bacterial wilt ([NT Government](https://nt.gov.au/industry/agriculture/food-crops-plants-and-quarantine/traditional-vegetables)).
**Organic sprays and products:** no treatment for an affected crop; none sourced.
**Evidence:** moderate to strong (government).
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Damping-off

**Recognise:** seed or seedling rots before emergence; after emergence a soft rot or water-soaked sunken lesion at ground level makes seedlings fall over ("wire stem" in cabbage); survivors are stunted ([Business Queensland](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/plants/diseases/horticultural/damping-off)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=damping+off+seedlings&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** raise seedlings in soil-less or fumigated mix on elevated benches, avoid overwatering and splash, clean tools and storage, thin for airflow (same source). The Hort Innovation guide adds raised beds or free-draining soil, transplants rather than direct seeding, rotation with non-hosts, organic matter, and weed control; it favours cool (about 13-15 C) waterlogged soil, and resting spores survive over 10 years ([Hort Innovation soilborne guide](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-soilborne-disease-in-vegetable-crops-guide.pdf)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** no source for Trichoderma or Bacillus seed treatments.
**Physical controls:** solarised potting mix was effective against Pythium myriotylum and Phytophthora nicotianae in NT trials ([NT Agnote 537](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/232921/537.pdf)); see [Soil solarisation](#soil-solarisation).
**Organic sprays and products:** the Queensland page covers no organic treatments.
**Evidence:** moderate (government). Sowing depth advice was not sourced.
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Root-knot nematode

**Recognise:** dig up the plant and check roots for galls or lumps. At-risk crops named: tomatoes and other solanaceae, cucurbits, carrots, lettuce, beans, pineapple, passionfruit ([Organic Gardener](https://www.organicgardener.com.au/biofumigation/)). It can spread on symptomless infested tubers, and patchy infestations call for soil testing by field section ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/?p=85173)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=root-knot+nematode+galls&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** rotate susceptible crops with non-hosts such as cereals or grasses; a fallow over six months can reduce some populations if kept weed-free but "may not reduce numbers below damage thresholds"; plant in late autumn or early spring when soil is cooler; inspect transplant roots; add organic matter and green manures (AUSVEG). In the NT, legume green manures are susceptible so should not precede susceptible vegetables ([NT Vegetable growing manual](https://industry.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/228007/ntg-vegetable-growing-manual.pdf)); the guava root-knot nematode concentrated in Darwin's residential suburbs infects guava, sunn hemp and many weeds, so practise "come clean, go clean" and contact plant.pathology@nt.gov.au ([NT DAF](https://daf.nt.gov.au/news/2026/root-knot-nematodes-in-the-nt)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced (Paecilomyces, Pochonia, neem cake and chitin were not found).
**Physical controls:** weed-free fallow (AUSVEG). Tagetes (French marigold) trial: in California field trials *T. patula* 'Single Gold' grown as a full-area prior crop consistently reduced root-knot infestation and galling on following tomato, equal to methyl iodide fumigation at one of two sites; tomato yield was about 50% higher than after fallow. This is cultivar-specific and non-Australian, and it works as a break crop, not as scattered companions ([PubMed 30818673](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30818673/); [Laidback Gardener](https://laidbackgardener.blog/2026/06/04/not-another-article-on-companion-planting-or-what-science-has-to-say-about-it/?amp=1), secondary, says small plantings in large fields are unlikely to matter). A Queensland glasshouse screen found sunn hemp resisted three Meloidogyne species, while forage sorghum 'Jumbo' and oat 'Swan' were highly resistant to all three; most soybeans and many biofumigants were susceptible ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/knowledge-hub/cover-crops-to-reduce-root-knot-nematode-populations/), glasshouse only). The NT source says sunn hemp hosts the guava root-knot nematode, so check which species you have (NT DAF above). In Queensland wet-tropics cane fallows no cover crop reduced pathogenic nematodes ([Soil CRC](https://soilcrc.com.au/?p=6583)).
**Organic sprays and products:** biofumigation: Organic Gardener says mustard (*Brassica carinata*) dug in before flower stems form will "significantly reduce" nematodes, but gives no measured results (weak). Caveats: a Gatton Qld trial did not test nematodes, gave inconsistent results for other soil pathogens and found fallow equal or better against Sclerotinia ([Duff and Firrell 2021](https://era.dpi.qld.gov.au/id/eprint/8750/1/J.-Duff-and-M.-Firrell-2021-Biofumigation--A-Cover-Crop-Option-12-Months-of-the-Year-to-Manage-Three-Soilborne-Pathogens-Ailing-the-Australian-Vegetable-Industry.pdf)); biofumigant brassicas can host brassica pests and diseases (same trial); and they raise clubroot risk (see [Clubroot](#clubroot)). An Alice Springs guide claims compost may cut nematodes by up to 90%; this is the guide's own claim, not trial data ([Alice Springs guide](https://assets.nationbuilder.com/aridlands/pages/4419/attachments/original/1726025047/Vegie_companion_small2.pdf)).
**Evidence:** weak to moderate. No single organic method eliminates it; marigold break crop is moderate but non-Australian; biofumigation is weak to moderate and conditional.
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Soil solarisation

**Recognise:** a physical method, tested in NT potting mix rather than in-ground beds.
**Prevent:** clear (not black) polyethylene, thinnest available (25-50 micron), mix kept moist, mounds about 25 cm; two layers with about 15 cm air gap hold more heat. Mix temperatures of 40-60 C greatly reduce or eliminate soil-borne diseases. Darwin trials controlled Pythium myriotylum, Phytophthora nicotianae and Sclerotium rolfsii in 3-9 days ([NT Agnote 537](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/232921/537.pdf)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** none.
**Physical controls:** September to mid-May temperatures reached mid-40s to 51 C under two layers; mid-May to September only about 41 C, so it is not fully effective; wet-season overcast lowers temperatures 4-5 C; UV-stabilised plastic lasts, non-UV about 4 weeks. Many weeds were controlled but some Amaranthus and Portulaca still germinated. Most results come from trials elsewhere (same source). In-ground depth of kill and nematode control are not sourced. Sheet plastic for solarisation is allowed under ACOS without certifier approval ([ACOS 2023](https://austorganic.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Australian-Certified-Organic-Standard-2023.pdf), partly read).
**Organic sprays and products:** none.
**Evidence:** moderate for NT potting mix.
**Sources:** [NT Agnote 537](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/232921/537.pdf).

## Nutrient and weather disorders

### Blossom end rot

**Recognise:** circular dead tissue at the blossom end of tomato, capsicum or watermelon, water-soaked then sunken and leathery; mould is secondary ([NT Agnote 479](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/233618/479.pdf)).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=blossom+end+rot+tomato&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** the direct cause is too little calcium reaching the fruit; soil calcium is usually adequate, so it is an uptake and distribution problem. Contributing factors: irregular water, hot dry windy weather, low calcium in light sandy or lateritic soils, very acid soil, excess nitrogen, high salts and waterlogging. Irrigate consistently (avoid long dry spells then heavy watering); add lime or calcium only where soil calcium is low, and raise pH carefully; avoid excessive nitrogen; improve drainage (same source). [inference] Mulch and steady drip watering are the first-line rescue.
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** as above.
**Organic sprays and products:** foliar calcium sprays and eggshells are not covered by the source, so their efficacy is ungraded.
**Evidence:** strong (government Agnote).
**Sources:** [NT Agnote 479](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/233618/479.pdf).

### Iron chlorosis and alkaline soil

**Recognise:** yellow-white leaves, notably in limestone coastal sands; alkaline soils may also cause shortages of iron, manganese, boron, copper and zinc ([DPIRD WA soil pH](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/soil-ph-and-plant-health), page marked possibly out of date).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=iron+chlorosis+leaves&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** a pH unit is a tenfold change, so go slowly; over-acidifying brings manganese and aluminium toxicity and phosphorus, calcium and magnesium deficiency (same source). Excess copper can itself cause iron chlorosis, so repeated copper sprays can contribute ([AIH](https://aih.org.au/?p=340)). In Alice Springs, irrigation raises soil pH over time and the guide suggests compost and sulphur and avoiding lime ([Alice Springs guide](https://assets.nationbuilder.com/aridlands/pages/4419/attachments/original/1726025047/Vegie_companion_small2.pdf)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** none.
**Organic sprays and products:** DPIRD WA: confirm with an iron chelate foliar spray (leaves green within a week), then mix iron sulphate into compost (1 cup per 10 L of moistened compost) and pack it into holes about 20 cm deep around the root zone. The WA page gives iron sulphate as its only acidifying method and does not mention elemental sulphur. Whether these inputs suit organic certification was not checked.
**Evidence:** moderate.
**Sources:** [DPIRD WA soil pH](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/soil-ph-and-plant-health), [AIH](https://aih.org.au/?p=340).

### Nitrogen magnesium and zinc shortage

**Recognise:** nitrogen: poor growth, older leaves pale green to yellow then dry and drop. Magnesium: older leaves with chlorotic patches between veins and a green triangle at the base, margins may burn. Zinc: stunted pale plants with creamy yellow areas between veins and distorted young leaves, youngest leaves first ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/?p=85200)).
**Prevent:** not sourced beyond the remedies below.
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** better irrigation helps nitrogen (same source).
**Organic sprays and products:** AUSVEG gives nitrogen fertiliser side-dressed before irrigation, dolomite or Epsom salts (or weekly foliar sprays) for magnesium, and zinc in basal fertiliser or foliar spray. No organic nitrogen source was specified, and whether Epsom salts or dolomite meet organic certification was not checked.
**Evidence:** moderate (industry source, vegetables).
**Sources:** [AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/?p=85200).

### Sunscald and heat

**Recognise:** sunburn comes from high temperature with strong infrared and UV ([AUSVEG/AgNova article](https://ausveg.com.au/?p=42978), promotional).

**See it:** [Photos: search Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=sunscald+fruit&title=Special%3AMediaSearch&type=image). Search results, not vetted.
**Prevent:** kaolin must be applied before heat events and wears off (same source).
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** shade cloth percentages and timing are not sourced.
**Organic sprays and products:** kaolin only, from a single company page with no efficacy data beyond a photo; its organic status was not checked.
**Evidence:** weak-anecdotal.
**Sources:** [AUSVEG/AgNova article](https://ausveg.com.au/?p=42978).

### Frost protection

**Recognise:** frost is most likely on calm clear nights, and young unhardened foliage is most vulnerable ([Hort Innovation VG13075](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg13075-blankets-for-vegetables.pdf)).
**Prevent:** [inference] choose planting dates after the local last frost; no source on site selection was found.
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** fleece (spun-bonded polypropylene) lifts air temperature around the crop about 2-5 C when air is up to 10 C; the effect roughly halves at windy sites. In trials babyleaf lettuce yield nearly tripled in frost at Camden, lettuce at Werribee gained almost 60% and capsicum at Bundaberg gained 29%. Fabric weight matters little; it is usually single-use; peg it carefully so it does not tear (same source). DPIRD WA frost research is broadacre-focused and not applicable to home gardens ([DPIRD WA](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/print/node/10040)).
**Organic sprays and products:** none sourced.
**Evidence:** moderate to strong (Hort Innovation trials). Frost recovery pruning is not sourced.
**Sources:** [Hort Innovation VG13075](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg13075-blankets-for-vegetables.pdf).

## Weeds

### Organic weed control

**Recognise:** no Australian source covered common garden weed identification (couch, kikuyu, nutgrass, oxalis and so on). For declared and invasive weeds see [Biosecurity and weeds](../biosecurity-and-weeds.md).
**Prevent:** control host weeds that carry disease: shepherd's purse and wild radish for clubroot ([Hort Innovation](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-clubroot-management-in-brassica-vegetables-fact-sheet.pdf)); keep fallows weed-free for root-knot nematode ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/?p=85173)). Mulch depth, cardboard mulching and hand weeding before seed set are standard practice but were not sourced.
**Beneficials and biological control:** none sourced.
**Physical controls:** in a NSW vineyard trial (three sites, one season) mulch worked well at Orange but not at Mudgee, flame gave some control at Orange but was problematic, and steam gave good suppression but was labour-intensive ([DPIRD NSW](https://dpird-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/__data/assets/pdf_file/0006/1158315/Alternative-weed-control-measures-for-vineyards.pdf), commercial vineyard context, so transfer to gardens is an inference). [inference] Flame weeding on dry vegetation carries fire risk: check fire restrictions and avoid total fire ban days (the vineyard report says fire restrictions limited flame trials elsewhere). Solarisation controls many annual and perennial weeds, though some Amaranthus and Portulaca survived ([NT Agnote 537](https://daf.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/232921/537.pdf)). PVC mulch products are not permitted under ACOS; other plastic mulch must be removed after use ([ACOS 2023](https://austorganic.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Australian-Certified-Organic-Standard-2023.pdf), partly read).
**Organic sprays and products:** vinegar is a short-lived burn-down. In the vineyard trial acetic acid and other desiccants killed broadleaf weeds within hours, but control was short-lived, grasses responded slowly and weeds re-emerged; pine oil shifted soil pH from 7.54 to 6.2 at Orange and sodium rose after sodium chloride and nonanoic acid (DPIRD NSW). Montana State Extension (US): household 5% vinegar kills only small seedlings (best at 2-4 leaves); 20-30% products work better but are strong acids needing eye and skin protection; perennials regrow from roots; there is no residual activity and it harms desirable plants ([Montana State Extension](https://www.montana.edu/extension/invasiveplants/extension/monthly-weed-posts/2021-june-does-vinegar-kill-weeds.html)). Copper cannot be used as a herbicide under ACOS (see [Copper and lime sulphur](#copper-and-lime-sulphur)).
**Evidence:** weak to moderate for vinegar (single US summary, contact burn-down only); mixed for mulch, flame and steam (one vineyard season).
**Sources:** as linked above.

## Soil problems

### Water-repellent sand

**Recognise:** waxy organic matter coats sand grains; sandy topsoils with under 5% clay are most at risk. Signs: dry patches between moist depressions, patchy or delayed emergence, runoff, and nutrient deficiencies (P, Cu, Zn, Mn) that fade after rain. High risk across 3.3 Mha of south-west WA and moderate on a further 6.9 Mha ([DPIRD WA overview](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/water-repellence/soil-water-repellence-overview?page=0%2C0), broadacre, page marked possibly out of date).
**Prevent:** [inference] the DPIRD page treats organic matter as the cause, not a fix, while Perth garden advice recommends it; this tension is unresolved in the sources reviewed.
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** broadacre options are clay spreading or delving (benefit up to 30 years; too much poorly mixed clay can cause haying off) and soil inversion or rotary spading (same source), which are machinery-scale. A school guide suggests bentonite at 6-8 kg/m2 dug into the top 25 cm, a rate the sources reviewed flag as looking high and not cross-checked ([WA Waste Authority school garden guide](https://www.wastesorted.wa.gov.au/assets/wss/resources/Toolkits%20and%20Factsheets/Edible_School_Garden_Guide.pdf), single non-specialist source).
**Organic sprays and products:** wetting agents give variable results; DPIRD banded rates are 0.5-2 L/ha and blanket 20-50 L/ha, with 0-20% yield responses, more reliable on some SW WA forest gravels ([DPIRD WA wetting agents](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/node/280), broadacre, possibly out of date). For Perth gardens, Water Corporation advises a wetting agent at planting, re-applied at the start of winter, early spring and early summer, organic matter into the top 30 cm in late autumn, and 5-10 cm of mulch ([Water Corporation](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/save-water/in-the-garden/improving-your-soil)). Garden-scale wetting agent doses and "natural" wetting agents were not sourced.
**Evidence:** weak to moderate for wetters (results inconsistent); moderate to strong for claying (broadacre).
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Acidic soil

**Recognise:** low pH reduces nutrient availability and microbial activity and raises aluminium and manganese toxicity (aluminium limiting from about pH 4.8); rhizobia are sensitive, with lupin the exception ([GRDC 2022](https://grdc.com.au/resources-and-publications/grdc-update-papers/tab-content/grdc-update-papers/2022/02/removing-yield-barriers-caused-by-acidity-and-preventing-subsoil-acidification)).
**Prevent:** NSW DPI targets pH(CaCl2) 5.5 for agricultural soils; GRDC gives 5.5-6.0 in one paper and "above 5.5" in another, both broadacre ([NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/agriculture/soils/guides/soil-acidity/soil-acidity-and-liming-2021-ac.19/how-do-i-manage-acidic-soils), [GRDC 2022b](https://grdc.com.au/resources-and-publications/grdc-update-papers/tab-content/grdc-update-papers/2022/08/soil-acidity-its-stratification-and-amelioration-with-lime)). CaCl2 reads about 0.7 below water pH (GRDC 2022), so do not mix the two scales. Brassicas for clubroot need pH 7.0-7.5, which is not a general target ([Hort Innovation](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-clubroot-management-in-brassica-vegetables-fact-sheet.pdf)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** none.
**Organic sprays and products:** use fine high-neutralising-value lime, evenly spread and incorporated; lime moves slowly (about 1-2 cm a year per GRDC), so apply at least a season before sowing sensitive crops; set rates from an accredited lab test; slaked lime is a building product, not standard agricultural lime (NSW DPI). GRDC warns that lime above about 2 t/ha on sand, 3 t/ha on sandy loam and 4 t/ha on loam or clay loam risks micronutrient deficiency, so split applications; these are broadacre rates and garden conversions are unverified.
**Evidence:** strong for broadacre guidance; garden thresholds not sourced.
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Salty soil and bore water

**Recognise:** leaf burn is the main symptom in home gardens ([DPIRD WA minimising salt damage](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/minimising-salt-damage?page=0%2C1), page last updated 2014 and under review). High salt causes drought-like stress and salt on sprayed leaves can scorch; most crops show no loss up to about 700 uS/cm ([NSW DPI saline irrigation](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/agriculture/soils/salinity/salinity-and-crops/saline-irrigation), farm irrigation).
**Prevent:** test bore water repeatedly after running the bore 10 minutes; blend or alternate bore and scheme water; keep salt-sensitive plants off saline bore reticulation; avoid muriate of potash (potassium sulphate has a lower salt index); do not water foliage with salty water in the day; drip pushes salts to the edge of the wetted area (DPIRD WA). Heavy mulch and deep waterings help in Alice Springs ([Alice Springs guide](https://assets.nationbuilder.com/aridlands/pages/4419/attachments/original/1726025047/Vegie_companion_small2.pdf)). Blossom end rot is linked to salt build-up (see [Blossom end rot](#blossom-end-rot)).
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** extra water (a leaching fraction, for example 16% for 1800 uS/cm water at 10% accepted loss) moves salts below the roots but needs good drainage (NSW DPI).
**Organic sprays and products:** on clay soils gypsum (up to 5 kg per square metre) helps flush salt (DPIRD WA). Liquid detergents have less salt than washing powders for grey water (DPIRD WA).
**Evidence:** moderate (government, but farm-focused or dated).
**Sources:** as linked above.

### Sodic clay and gypsum

**Recognise:** sodosols have clay subsoils that are dispersive and restrict roots, water and air ([Agriculture Victoria VRO](https://vro.agriculture.vic.gov.au/DPI/Vro/vrosite.nsf/pages/hort_vic_soils-sodosol)).
**Prevent:** raise surface organic matter, reduce cultivation, retain residues, work soil at optimum moisture, use raised beds and drainage, avoid hardpans (same source).
**Beneficials and biological control:** not applicable.
**Physical controls:** raised beds and drainage.
**Organic sprays and products:** gypsum helps sodic, dispersive clays only, and the rate comes from a lab test ([CCMA](https://soilhealth.ccmaknowledgebase.vic.gov.au/SoilHealthResource/documents/outputs/education_and_training/Dispersive%20Soils%20-%20Agriculture%20Note.pdf)). DPIRD WA's broadacre table runs from 0-1.5 t/ha at slight dispersion on neutral or acid soils up to 5 t/ha for severe; apply with caution to gardens ([DPIRD WA](https://www.agric.wa.gov.au/node/5152)). [inference] Gypsum and lime follow a soil test and are different fixes for different problems.
**Evidence:** moderate (government, broadacre).
**Sources:** as linked above.

## Products and inputs explained

### Potassium bicarbonate

- **Label (one Australian product, 940 g/kg):** powdery mildew on tomatoes, capsicums, cucumbers, strawberries and zucchini at 30-40 g per 10 L water plus 20 mL emulsified vegetable oil, every 7-10 days; roses (powdery mildew and black spot) 40 g per 10 L plus 20 mL oil, every 10-14 days. Mix with the container half-filled, add the product while stirring, use within 24 hours. Contact with the disease is essential; avoid spraying stressed plants; no withholding period when used as directed. The table in the extracted label was ambiguous, so confirm the rate on the physical label and check APVMA registration ([OCP Eco-Fungicide label](https://ozbreed.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/eco-fungicide-500g-label4.4.18.pdf)).
- **Trial evidence:** moderate, one greenhouse cucumber trial ([Hort Innovation VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf)). AWRI names it as an alternative to sulphur in organic vineyards ([AWRI](https://www.awri.com.au/information_services/ebulletin/2021/10/13/agrochemical-update-october-2021-using-sulfur-to-protect-vines-particularly-under-cool-conditions/)).
- **ACOS:** listed as allowed in the excerpt read (see [Organic certification](#organic-certification-acos-partly-read)).

### Sulphur

- **Label (one Australian product, 800 g/kg):** do not use on sulphur-sensitive crops above 24 C; do not apply in the heat of the day or hot weather; do not mix with oils or apply within 3 weeks of oil sprays; no withholding period. The rates are agricultural, and the extracted label text was scrambled, so verify on the physical label ([OCP Ecosulfur label](https://ocp.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ecosulfur-label-frontback.pdf)). No home-garden dose was sourced.
- **Heat:** AWRI (grapes) says sulphur works best at about 25-30 C and phytotoxicity risk rises above 32 C when humidity is over 70%; coverage is critical because it kills spores on contact. The 24 C label figure and the 32 C AWRI figure differ because they refer to different crops ([AWRI](https://www.awri.com.au/information_services/ebulletin/2021/10/13/agrochemical-update-october-2021-using-sulfur-to-protect-vines-particularly-under-cool-conditions/)).
- **Other cautions:** not suitable for all crops, especially in hot weather ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf)); little effect once infection has begun ([Hort Innovation](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-biopesticides-in-australia-fact-sheet.pdf)); higher rates can harm beneficial organisms (AWRI). It was not tested in the cucumber trial; wettable sulphur and sulphur pots were only described as grower practice, not endorsed ([VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf)).

### Copper and lime sulphur

- **Accumulation:** an Australian vineyard survey (98 vineyards, 10 regions) found 96% had elevated copper compared with nearby native soil, generally below levels lethal to soil invertebrates but above levels causing sublethal effects ([Infowine summary](https://www.infowine.com/en/copper-fungicide-residues-in-australian-vineyard-soils/)). Copper builds up in soil, is toxic to soil organisms and aquatic life, does not break down in organic matter, and can cause iron chlorosis ([AIH](https://aih.org.au/?p=340), professional-body page, weak to moderate). Hort Innovation says do not spray copper at low pH (phytotoxicity) and that accumulated copper and phosphites can degrade soils ([Hort Innovation](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-biopesticides-in-australia-fact-sheet.pdf)).
- **Limit:** under ACOS, "on an average rolling basis, copper application up to 6 kg/Ha/annum is permissible as a restricted input" (R), and copper cannot be used as a herbicide ([ACOS 2023](https://austorganic.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Australian-Certified-Organic-Standard-2023.pdf), partly read). That is a certified-farm limit; no Australian home-garden annual limit was found.
- **Use:** use sparingly, rotate with cultural controls, apply before bud development, and remember fungicides do not cure existing infections (AIH). AgriFutures says organic standards "have limited the use of copper" ([AgriFutures](https://agrifutures.com.au/product/alternatives-to-copper-for-disease-control-in-the-australian-organic-industry/)); its findings were not read.
- **Lime sulphur:** SGA prefers it to copper for peach leaf curl because copper accumulates ([SGA](https://www.sgaonline.org.au/peach-leaf-curl/)). ACOS lists it as restricted: foliar use as fungicide or insecticide is limited, and other products should be considered where beneficial insects may be affected (ACOS).

### Horticultural oils

- Oils were the best eradicants of powdery mildew in the cucumber trial but lost effect after about a day; a petroleum oil was the best protectant for up to 7 days ([Hort Innovation VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf)). Do not combine with sulphur: the sulphur label forbids oil within 3 weeks. Vegetable oils are listed as allowed in ACOS (excerpt read). Vegetable use of white oil is by minor use permit only per the insect-pest notes; check the home-garden pack label ([insect pests](../pests/insects.md)).

### Compost tea

- **Evidence is mixed (weak to moderate).** An AUSVEG/Hort Innovation report reviewed overseas data: manure compost tea matched dichlofluanid on bean leaves in one review; weekly tea cut grey mould on greenhouse tomatoes by 30 and 37% but less than a fungicide; powdery mildew control was partial. Teas fermented 14 days or more did better; sterile or filtered extracts had little effect; quality was highly variable with no standard method ([AUSVEG VG99076](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/VG99076.pdf)).
- **Safety:** farm-made tea showed thermotolerant coliforms up to 1,600 per 100 mL in some batches (Salmonella not detected). The report calls for Australian field trials and says tea sprayed on food crops needs pathogen testing; do not use manure-based tea on edible parts without composting and testing (same source).
- **ACOS:** compost tea must be made from allowed materials with an AS 4454-compliant composting process ([ACOS 2023](https://austorganic.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Australian-Certified-Organic-Standard-2023.pdf), partly read).

### Milk spray

- In the cucumber trial 10% full-fat milk had less mildew two weeks after spraying, but the difference was not statistically significant. The authors suggest milk may act as a protectant rather than an eradicant ([Hort Innovation VG05094](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/historic-reports/sustainable-integrated-control-of-foliar-diseases-in-greenhouse-vegetables-vg05094.pdf)). Evidence: weak. A Wine Australia grape report surfaced in search but was not read.

### Bacillus products

- Hort Innovation describes microbial biopesticides (Bt strains are over 90% of commercial ones) and warns that results are variable, need thorough repeat coverage, and that many are registered for broadacre first with fewer vegetable registrations; it did not mention Bacillus subtilis ([Hort Innovation](https://www.horticulture.com.au/globalassets/hort-innovation/resource-assets/vg15010-biopesticides-in-australia-fact-sheet.pdf)). Bacillus subtilis at transplanting helped against clubroot only at low pressure (see [Clubroot](#clubroot)). AUSVEG says no registered or consistently effective biocontrol exists for the mildews ([AUSVEG](https://ausveg.com.au/app/data/technical-insights/docs/MegaPestsManagingFoliarDiseases.pdf)).
- Which Bacillus subtilis products are registered for home gardens, and their ACOS status, are unverified. Evidence: weak and unverified. For Bt against caterpillars see [insect pests](../pests/insects.md).

### Organic certification (ACOS, partly read)

> **Safety:** the Australian Certified Organic Standard details here come from a document that was only partly read. The two passes together read roughly characters 100,000-300,000 of a 362,331-character PDF, plus some earlier text on composting and mulch; Annex I was read only as an excerpt, and text after character 300,000 was not read. Certified growers must check each product against the standard and their certifier. Home gardeners are not bound by it.

- Plant-protection inputs must be Annex I active ingredients listed A (allowed) or R (restricted); formulated products with other active ingredients need written certifier approval. In the excerpt: sulphur (A), potassium bicarbonate (A), Bacillus thuringiensis (A), vegetable oils (A), beneficial organisms (A), compost tea (A, conditions above); copper (R, 6 kg/ha/year average); lime sulphur (R); microbial products (R, verified non-GM and free of synthetic preservatives); plant extracts (R, natural sources only) ([ACOS 2023](https://austorganic.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Australian-Certified-Organic-Standard-2023.pdf)).
- Compost must follow AS 4454 (2012): heap at 55 C for three continuous days in a static aerated pile, or 15 days in a windrow with at least five turns; about 20 t/ha/year composted manure is an acceptable guide. "Naturally occurring mineral nitrogen fertiliser products are prohibited" (same source).
- Bacillus subtilis and other Bacillus species were not named in the excerpt, so their status is unverified. "Allowed" means certifiable, not proven to work.
